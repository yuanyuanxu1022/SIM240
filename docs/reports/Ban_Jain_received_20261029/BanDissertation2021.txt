       Nanostructures and nanomaterials are means to dramatically improve the

performance of LEDs, solar cells, hard disk drives, laser diodes and displays. This

improvement comes from fabricating nanostructures inside or on the surface of a substrate.

Fabrication of these nanostructures often involve coating and imprinting of thin films onto

a substrate. However, defects produced during the manufacturing process destroy the

functionality of nanostructures. Thus, the fluid dynamics of thin fluid films involved in

manufacturing process is an interesting scientific and engineering problem. The first

system studied in this dissertation is the UVNIL. It is a promising high-resolution

nanopatterning process and a key technique for the commercialization of nanostructure

application. However, the UVNIL suffers from low throughput. The bottleneck is the

resist filling step, so the fluid flow in UVNIL has been studied extensively to find the

minimum time required for completing filling of features can be found by studying the

flow behavior of resist. The second system is a thin film resist used in lithography industry.

Thin liquid coatings, which have thicknesses of tens of nanometers, are frequently used in

lithography. However, generating ultra-thin films is challenging because thinner films are
                                           vi
more susceptible to defects and disturbances. Efforts to model the evolution of film profile

have been made and flow and leveling dynamics of thin film were modelled successfully.

1.1 HISTORICAL CONTEXT OF INTEGRATED CIRCUITS

       In 1946, the first programmable electronic computer, the Electronic Numerical

Integrator and Computer (ENIAC) was developed [1]. The ENIAC was developed for

calculating firing tables for the Ballistic Research Laboratory, but its first application was

for the Manhattan Project [1]. The ENIAC was built with approximately 18,000 vacuum

tubes and 1500 relays. These vacuum tubes, which controls electric current flow in a

vacuum, consumed a large amount of power, suffered frequent failure, thus required

frequent replacement [1, 2]. Vacuum tubes could not support evolution of complex

electronic circuits, because the vacuum tubes are too big, unreliable, and consuming too

much power [3]. A huge step forward for miniaturized electronic circuits came with

development of the transistors [3, 4]. The transistors, instead of using electrons in a

vacuum, rely on the nearly free electrons in the solid-state materials known as

semiconductors. As a discrete device, the transistors replaced the vacuum tube because of

a big reduction in power and failure rate. In a few years, the technique for making

transistors made a steady process, eventually overwhelmed the digital technology [2, 3, 5].

       Despite the miniaturization of the transistor, the initial size of electronic circuits

was quite large, and reducing overall circuit size, weight, and power dissipation, as well as

better performance, has remained as a major objective of electronics research. Because

individual components made of different materials and fabrication processes, each

components was soldered to be connected, and soldering was labor-intensive and hindering

the miniaturization of electronic circuits [3, 5]. Kilby provided an alternative approach to

the challenge by reducing both the number of materials used and fabrication processes.

                                              1
Kilby’s solution was to fabricate all components within a single piece of material, since

other components, such as resistors and capacitors, could be made from semiconductors.

The new design for the circuit allowed the components to be made with on process

sequence and to be packed closer together with fewer complex interconnections and failure

mechanisms [3, 5].

        Separately and about the same time, Noyce came up with a similar design which

became known as the monolithic integrated circuit. His approach was more practical for

fabrication by using a planar semiconducting substrate with oxidation and diffusion

processes to create the circuits and used evaporated metal to form the connections between

circuits.   The planar process revolutionized the way electronics were made and the

complexity of functions that could be combined into a single circuit. Thereby, circuit

volume, power dissipation, and wiring parasitic effects were dramatically reduced. After

the revolution in circuit miniaturization, the demand for cost-effective memory devices and

integrated circuits for computers generate the semiconductor industry in the 1970s [6]. The

global semiconductor integrated circuits sales reached $360.27 Billion in 2020 and the

industry is expected to grow more[7]. Since their origins, the speed, and capacity of

integrated circuits have progressed enormously; driven by the ability to pattern small

features in order to fit more and more transistors on chips of the same size [6].

1.2 PHOTOLITHOGRAPHY: HISTORY, FUTURE AND CHALLENGES

        After the development of the integrated circuit, the fabrication process evolved

drastically. Improvement in resolution of fabrication process has been the main driving

force, because a better resolution allows miniaturization of electronic components, which

results in more chips per wafer, smaller circuit size, and higher performance devices.

Furthermore, a better resolution improves productivity and thus reduces cost. Thus, the

                                             2
fabrication process requires the ability to pattern complex structures at high resolution

yield and throughput [8]. Photolithography has been the dominant patterning process,

which has until recently kept up with the stringent technology requirements of integrated

circuit manufacturing process [9].




 Figure 1.1: Schematic of photolithography process. A spin coated substrate is
             prepared. The photoresist is selectively illuminated through the unmasked
             regions. A positive resist becomes soluble in the illuminated areas whereas
             the negative resist becomes insoluble in the illuminated areas. The
             development process washes soluble resist, an etch step transfers the pattern
             into the device material, and the photoresist can be stripped off to leave the
             patterned circuitry on the wafer.


                                             3
       Figure 1.1 shows a schematic of the general process of photolithography. A

photomask engraved with a circuit pattern is placed over a substrate coated with a

photoresist. The photoresist is exposed to light through the photomask, and the exposed

regions are chemically changed. The exposed area becomes insoluble if it is a negative

photoresist or soluble if it is a positive resist. Then, the photoresist is immersed in a

developer solution that dissolves the soluble areas, and the pattern from the mask is

replicated on to the substrate by using remaining photoresist as an etch barrier. With these

basic concepts, the photolithography process evolved into the highest resolution, most

accurate patterning process [9].




 Figure 1.2: Conceptual schematics of contact, proximity and projection printing.

       In the exposure step, the photolithography process has evolved from contact

printing to proximity printing in order to protect a mask from the contact (Fig. 1.2). The

proximity printing process further evolved into projection printing to alleviate resolution

limit caused by near-field diffraction. Although only few improvements in apparatus were

highlighted in Figure 1.2, there were many other upgrades in the photolithography process.

                                             4
For example, as chip became more complex, there was advancement in mask making

process. As resolution requirements continuously grew, reduction lithography, which

prints a reduced image on the wafer, was developed. Reduction lithography eliminated

making 1:1 1 masks, but required an additional step which aligns the reduced image onto

the wafer thus improvements in metrology were inevitably required. Furthermore, as

resolution and image area grew, this new process put a burden on lens manufacture by

requiring dramatic increases in lens size and complexity. Lastly, changing the illumination

source was necessary for a better resolution, which required a change of optical materials,

photoresist materials, chemistry and processes, as well as the improvement development

tools [9].

        Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography is one of next generation lithography, which

followed the above development process with several unexperienced challenges [8]. EUV

lithography enhanced resolution by using soft X-ray wavelength. First, EUV lithography

tools must operate in high vacuum and only incorporate reflective optical elements

(including photomasks), because EUV wavelengths cannot penetrate through significantly

any medium. Since every EUV reflective surface absorbs ~ 30%, creation of a high-

powered EUV light source is necessary. Since photons coming from high power EUV

sources can severely heat the tool, thermal stability of pellicles and heat extraction from

vacuum raise engineering challenges. As can be seen, improvement in photolithography

is challenge because each component in lithography tool is interconnected [8].

1.3 ULTRAVIOLET NANOIMPRINT LITHOGRAPHY: BACKGROUND AND HIGHLIGHTS

        Table 1.1 lists intricate requirements a new lithographic technology attain to be

considered as a viable manufacturing option for advanced semiconductor integrated

circuits [8]. For 50 years, optical lithography has been the dominant patterning process for

                                             5
semiconductor industry [9]. Even though diffraction limits the ultimate resolution of

photolithography, replacing photolithography with a disruptive technology has proven to

be difficult [8]. A new lithographic technology needs to address the fundamental resolution

limits of 193i photolithography [8].       In order to address resolution limits of 193i

photolithography, the industry has explored several other high-resolution lithography

techniques [8].

        While several nanopatterning techniques have been proposed by researchers, EUV

lithography and ultraviolet nanoimprint lithography (UVNIL) are the only ones which have

commercial potential for semiconductor production [8]. Nanoimprint lithography (NIL) is

an emerging technique which is an effective and low-cost nanoscale patterning method and

a promising solution to the practical limits of photolithography [6, 8]. A general NIL

process is illustrated in Figure. 1.3. Before imprinting, the imprint resist is dispensed on

the substrate. The template mask is lowered onto the monomer droplets of resist, and the

droplets spread and fill the relief patterns in the mask by capillary action. The resist drops

merge and completely fill the features on the template. A photo-curable or thermally

curable polymer resist reproduces the pattern after curing. The template mask is removed,

leaving an imprinted resist on the substrate. Ideally, imprinted resist conforms completely

with original template. This pattern can then be used for further processing such as reactive

ion etch. The NIL process offers a low cost method for mass producing nanoscale features

and has the potential to become a key nanolithography method for future manufacturing of

integrated circuits.

pattern resolution in UVNIL is essentially only limited by the resolution of the template

step-and-repeat and roll-to-roll can be combined. Instead of moving the template, it is

1.4 CHALLENGES FOR UVNIL: DEFECTIVITY, THROUGHPUT, AND DILEMMA

       While UVNIL has been demonstrated its use in the hard disk industry[27, 28] and

display photonics[29] as patterning tools, there are several challenges in improving

throughput and defectivity that need to be addressed in order to be more successfully

implemented in the industry. The challenges involved in these metrics and relationship

between the two metrics are discussed in this section.
                                            10
        The particular system studied in this dissertation is Jet and Flash Imprint

Lithography (J-FIL) which implemented UVNIL in the step-and-repeat configuration. J-

FIL deposits a low viscosity resist onto a rigid substrate. The template moves to each field

of the rigid substrate, lowered into the fluid which flows into the template pattern. After

filling, the resist is exposed to UV radiation and cross-linked. The template is separated

and leaving a patterned resist and moved another field to imprint. Figure 1.5 shows a wafer

patterned using UVNIL process. The left figure shows partial field, labelled as P, and full

field, labelled as F, of the wafer. UVNIL process need to perfectly imprint rectangular

shape full fields with no fluid extrusions outside the rectangular fields and no gap between

adjacent fields. Further, there are partial fields, which are asymmetrically shaped fields, at

the edge of wafer. These partial fields are important for yielding purpose because these

partial fields contain >10% of produced devices [8]. The middle figure (Fig. 1.5) shows

defects after UVNIL process, and the left figure (Fig. 1.5) shows perfectly imprinted

patterns after the calibration.

        Defects during pattern transfer process strongly impact the device performance thus

directly related to the cost. Acceptance of UVNIL for patterning process will require a

demonstration of defect levels commensurate with cost-effective device production.

Possible sources of defects are non-filling of features and inadequate UV curing. There

are five possible cases of non-filling defects can occur; (i) bubbles created by air trapped

in the resist material, (ii) void defects created by inadequate UV curing, (iii) defects created

during the separation, (iv) void defects created by inadequate resist dispense, and (v) void

defects caused by insufficient time taken during filling step. Chauhan et al. analyzed the

first type of defect in a rigid substrate by simulating the feature filling phenomenon [30].

More specifically, diffusion of a gas, entrapped in the template features, through liquid

imprint resist was studied. The simulation of feature filling including gas dissolution
                                         11
during the UVNIL was developed to quantify feature filling time. Simulation results

revealed two important trends. First is the smaller the feature size, faster the filling because

of capillary pressure, and this characteristic makes UVNIL more suitable for nano-

patterning process. Second the diffusion of entrapped gas fast enough, thus the gas

diffusion is not a cause for non-filling of features [30].

        For UVNIL with rigid substrates, the exposure time and template/resist separation

steps are well understood and typical time for these steps is 0.1 to 0.2 seconds [31]. In

UVNIL with stepper template, non-filling occurs mainly due to long filling times required

to spread and fill the features and gap between the template and the substrate [32]. Thus,

in this dissertation, void defects created by inadequate resist dispense and void defects

caused by insufficient time taken during filling step are main topics.

        Throughput is another key challenge in UVNIL. A recent cost of ownership

calculations shows that an UVNIL tool system needs to produce 15 wafers per hour at a

defect density of 0.1 defects/cm2 to compete with a single ArH self-aligned quadruple

                                              12
patterning tool [33]. Throughput includes overhead, resist dispense, spread time, exposure

and separation (Fig. 1.6).      As mentioned before, typical time for exposure and

template/resist separation steps is only 0.1 to 0.2 seconds. The template fluid filling takes

the largest part in the throughput budget (Fig. 1.6) and is known to be the bottleneck in

UVNIL [34].




 Figure 1.6: Throughput budget breakdown. To achieve a throughput of 80 wafers per
             hour, a resist filling time of 1.1 seconds is required. (adapted from
             Khusnatdinov et al. [34]).

       Although defect rate and throughput are presented as separate metrics, the two

metrics are inseparable in UVNIL because the time required for the resist fluid to

completely fill a template is important for both defectivity and throughput of UVNIL.
Insufficient filling time leads to incomplete filling and gas bubbles which become defects

after the curing process. Excessive filling time may reduce defects but reduces throughput.

Thus defectivity needs to be considered in the context of throughput, and both must be

addressed simultaneously.

       In implementing UVNIL, there is a dilemma between the defect density and

throughput. Furthermore, stringent requirements and cost of ownership demand UVNIL

to be both defect free and high throughput process. Fig. 1.7B suggests that the optimized
                                           13
droplet placements can resolve the dilemma. The local fluid flow is a function of pattern

type, viscosity, and wetting behavior. As can be seen in Fig. 1.7A, flow pattern changes

as the shape of a template changes. The graph in Fig. 1.7B further illustrates the optimized

dispense of imprint resist droplets is not only reduces defects density but also increases

throughput by reducing the required spreading time.              Additionally, imprint fluid

distribution is also important to achieve thin and uniform residual layer thickness (RLT).

The thin and uniform RLT is important to enable subsequent etching. The imprinted

replica serves as a sacrificial etch mask, which helps to transfer the pattern to a film on the

wafer. If RLT is not thin and uniform, the pattern cannot be accurately transferred to the

film. If the fluid distribution is accurately matched the pattern density variation, the RLT

beneath the pattern is thin and uniform [8].

        In summary, a key to the successful deployment of UVNIL to nano-patterning

process will need to break the bottleneck of UVNIL. There are several experimental

observations and analysis which indicates template fluid filling, the bottleneck, is strongly

related to the structure of a template and the configuration of droplet placement. Therefore,

through the insights gained from a comprehensive model which captures major physics

involved with fluid flow, rational design of a template or a droplet pattern could be

employed in the future to achieve a low defectivity and high throughput UVNIL process.

Thus, the main research objective in this dissertation is finding an optimal UVNIL process

setting which run at the shortest fluid filling time that leads to an acceptable defect density.

1.5 MODELING AND SIMULATION IN UVNIL

       While it is possible to find the optimal process window of UVNIL with

experiments, it is unrealistic, expensive and laborious to optimize for every new template

design. Instead, computer simulations offer a faster and inexpensive means to optimizing

droplet placements. Also, modeling and simulation tool can prove insights about the

physical phenomena involved feature filling time of UVNIL process. Computational tools

have been successful in the past for studying the filling steps in UVNIL and some of them

are presented here. There are several studies that compare the theoretical model with

experimental results [36-39]. These four papers have different nanoimprint experimental

schemes, but uses a similar coarse-grain method to describe the resist viscous flow. In

many, models are based on an equation that specifies the 2D pressure distribution for a

given resist thickness and a template velocity and eliminate vertical resist motion. These

models were used to predict residual thickness, pressure and velocity inside of resist fluid.

Also, simulations have shown a satisfying qualitative agreement in predicting pressure

                                             15
inside of resist fluid and used to predict the filling times. These models take advantage of

disparate length scales. The width of the imprinted field is greater than the gap height

between a substrate and a template, lubrication theory approximations can be applied to

the continuity and momentum equation that describe the fluid flow behavior.

       Another set of studies focused on fluid flow in a feature scale level. These studies

simulate resist interface advancements through template features. In Figure 1.8, Reddy et

al. showed time evolutions of the fluid-air interface as it moves through a feature under

low Capillary number [40]. They were able to predict possible incomplete feature filling

cases for a given channel height, feature height and feature width. Morihara et al. and

Nagaoka et al. studied the bubble trapping mechanism in respect to contact angles [41, 42].

The key finding of these studies is shown in Figure 1.9, which shows distribution of

evaluated cases in respect to contact angles. The studies claims that large contact angle

between the resist and the template causes bubble trapping, however the contact angle

between the resist and the substrate does not have great influence.

       During UVNIL process physical phenomena called pinning can occur when the

pressure at the air-liquid interface reaches the pressure of the bulk liquid as shown in Figure

1.10. At this condition, there is no pressure gradient or driving force to move the liquid-air

interface. The pinning can be both beneficial and disadvantageous to UNVIL process.

Since the fluid interface would not move, the pinning can prevent extrusion and provide a

time window for the filling and curing steps [8, 43]. However, an arrested fluid-air

interface can lead to the formation of bubbles and cause defects. Chauhan et al. examined

several parameters and their effects on pinning, presented a theory of pinning of interface,

and proposed a modification on a template design which facilitate the filling of features
without any extrusion. There is another important study by Chauhan et al [30]. Chauhan

et al examined diffusion of gas entrapped in the features. Their simulation predicts a rapid

capillary pressure controlled filling of features initially followed by a slower diffusion

controlled process. They also found that the absolute time for diffusion of a gas, entrapped

in features is very short and gas diffusion will not cause non-filling of features during the

UVNIL [30].

1.6 OPEN CHALLENGES AND RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

       Nanostructures and nanomaterials are means to dramatically improve the

performance of LEDs, solar cells, hard disk drives, laser diodes and displays. This

improvement comes from fabricating nanostructures inside or on the surface of a substrate.

Fabrication of these nanostructures often involve coating and imprinting of thin films onto

a substrate. However, defects produced during the manufacturing process destroy the

                                             19
functionality of nanostructures.   Thus, the fluid dynamics of thin films involved in

manufacturing process is an interesting scientific and engineering problem.

       The UVNIL a promising high-resolution nanopatterning process and a key

technique for the commercialization of nanostructure application. However, the UVNIL

suffers from low throughput. The bottleneck is the resist filling step, so the fluid flow in

UVNIL has been studied extensively to find the minimum time required for completing

filling of features can be found by studying the flow behavior of resist. It has been

experimentally shown that optimizing the locations and sizes of droplets is the key to

reducing defects and increasing throughput of UVNIL [35]. Efforts to model the imprint

fluid flow in UVNIL also have been made. From model simulations, it was found that the

imprint time decreased with increasing number of droplets [44, 45]. Also, from the model

simulations, square arrangement is the optimal placement for flat template structure of

isotropic flow [45].   Furthermore, it was found that droplet placement error during

dispensing step can increase the filling time [45]. However, in previous studies, capillary

pressure arises from structured template was roughly approximated and anisotropic flow

caused by template structures were underestimated. Also, simulations are mainly carried

out under a template with uniform structure. However, in practice templates are composed

of multiple structure, so optimizing fluid flow under multiple patterned template remained

unanswered.

       Another system studied in this dissertation is healing or dewetting of a defect on a

thin film. In lithography industry, thin liquid coatings, which have thicknesses of tens of

nanometers, are frequently used. However, generating ultra-thin films is challenging

because thinner films are more susceptible to defects and disturbances. The dynamics of

think liquid film received significant scientific attention and studied with computational

models and experiments. The growth of small defects in a nano-scale thin film was
                                      20
captures experimentally [47]. The leveling dynamics of hole in a thin film was also

experimentally studied using atomic force microscopy [48]. Efforts to model the evolution

of film profile have been made and flow and leveling dynamics of thin film were modelled

successfully [49, 50]. Although the dynamics of dewetting and healing of film have been

extensively studied, an important and unstudied case is the effect of Marangoni forces. The

Marangoni effect is the fluid flow from regions of lower surface tension to regions of higher

surface tension. The computational study presented in this dissertation will provide insight

into the reach physics involved in described thin film flow dynamics.

       The remainder of the dissertation is organized as follows. In Chapter 2 the capillary

pressure arose from the nanostructures of a template is studied carefully and incorporated

into UVNIL fluid flow simulations. The anisotropic flow caused the template structures is

studied extensively and optimal placement for line and space pattern was studied. In

Chapter 3 UVNIL fluid flow under multiple patterns was studied, furthermore the

versatility of the hexagonal arrangement was studied in various template structure. In

Chapter 4 the healing and dewetting dynamics of a hole in a thin film is studied and critical

ratio of film thickness and defect size was analyzed. The evolution of thin film profile

under the influence of Marangoni effects was studied. Also, the critical Marangoni

numbers which switch the dewetting cases into a healing case was found. Finally, in

Chapter 5, concluding remarks were made and future work was presented.

2.1 INTRODUCTION

        Implementation of nanotechnologies depends on the ability to pattern small

features, and the required feature sizes are continuously shrinking [1].                    Ultraviolet

nanoimprint lithography (UVNIL) is a promising high-resolution molding process.

UVNIL exceeds the limit of 193 nm lithography, the current dominant technology. Also,

UVNIL has several advantages compare to the next generation extreme ultraviolet

lithography (EUVL) [1].          UVNIL has high resolution and the resolution is largely

unaffected by the field size being patterned; UVNIL is essentially only limited by the

resolution of the template pattern [2, 3].              UVNIL offers a low-cost alternative to

photolithography and EUVL. It avoids the use of costly optical systems for traditional

photolithography.      When compared to an immersion based Self Aligned Quadruple

Patterning (SAQP), cost of ownership for UVNIL is approximately 40% lower[4]. Lastly,

using inkjet dispenser, the fluid is dispensed as an individual droplet. Because the resist

volume deposited is matched to the mask pattern density, there is no wasted resist, and the

resulting total volume of resist liquid dispensed on a substrate is around 0.001 times smaller

than a more conventional spin-coat type resist [4]. The ability of UVNIL to cheaply and

reliably replicate nanoscale features is potentially a tremendous asset to many applications

[1].

        A typical process of UVNIL is shown in Fig. 2.1. An inkjet dispenser is used to

dispense droplets of low viscosity photo-curable imprint material on the substrate. These

droplets are a few picoliters in volume. A UV-transparent template with micro- or nano-

substrates and deployed in hard disk industry and photonics applications [5, 6].

                                             27
       Despite its advantages, defects caused by gas trapping and low throughput make

UVNIL less charming [1, 7]. Several processing steps are involved with the throughput

and defectivity of UV-NIL. Most of these steps are well-established, but the feature filling

step needs more understanding and still has much room for improvement [7]. The chart

shown in Fig. 2.1 shows the total throughput budget in UVNIL. About 30% is taken by

resist dispensing, alignments, UV radiation, and peeling. The rest of the budget taken by

fluid filling step. These steps have to be completed in ~1.5 seconds or less for a competitive

manufacturing process [6]. It is obvious from this chart that the fluid filling is the

bottleneck in UVNIL. It is possible to increase the throughput by reducing the filling time.

This may increase the number for wafer produced per hour, but at the same time, defects

in imprinted pattern increase because of incomplete filling. The complete fluid filling is

achieved only when all the voids have disappeared, and the challenge is finding the shortest

time required to achieve complete filling. Thus, in UVNIL defectivity and throughput are

related to each other and defectivity needs to be considered in the context of throughput,

and both must be addressed simultaneously. There is a need to understand the optimal

droplet arrangement because optimizing the locations and sizes of droplets is the key to

reduce defects and increase the throughput of UVNIL [6]. Thus, fluid management is an

important issue in enhancing UVNIL process. Therefore, through the insights gained from

a comprehensive model which captures major physics involved with fluid flow, rational

design of a droplet pattern could be employed in the future to achieve a low defectivity and

high throughput UVNIL process.

       The feature filling step can be mathematically described using lubrication theory

[8]. By predictive modeling the fluid flow during the feature filling step, high throughput

and low defectivity UVNIL method can be developed. Previously, a simulation model

describing the flow and coalescence of the multiple fluid drops was developed [8]. This
                                           28
initial model was used to study a blank template with no features. Afterward, a simulation

method which describes the fluid flow under line and space pattern template [9]. Flow in

high density line and space patterns is simulated by calculating the effective permeability

for templates [9]. From previous studies, it is found that the imprint time is shown to

decrease with increasing numbers of drops, the placement errors increases filling time, and

the directionality of the pattern dictates the spreading of the droplets [8-10].

       However, previous studies did not incorporate the capillary pressure generated by

structures of UVNIL template.         Since UVNIL templates have a high density of

nanostructures, it is important to consider the flow along small grooves and the resulting

capillary pressure.    Thus, this study explored the capillary pressure resulting from

nanostructures. Surface Evolver [11] was used to numerically calculate the fluid-air

interface, and a simple physical model to approximate the capillary pressure was

developed. The simple capillary pressure model did not add a complexity to the coarse-

grained model but only added accuracy to the simulation results. Using the accurate

simulation method, droplet spreading patterns and filling time are studied. Also, various

comparisons are made among different template dimensions and different droplet

arrangements. From the comparison, the most effective droplet arrangement for a line and

space template is determined. The objectives are to understand the effect of anisotropic

capillary pressure, and droplet arrangement. Furthermore, we present the general rule of

thumb in determining the optimal droplet arrangement is also discussed.

2.2 SIMULATION METHOD

       Optimizing the locations and sizes of droplets is the key to reducing defects and

increasing throughput of UVNIL, simulation models describing the flow and coalescence

of the multiple fluid drops were developed and used to predict the fluid flow during

                                              29
spreading and feature filling. More specifically, an accurate method to estimate capillary

pressure boundary conditions at the fluid-air interface is developed along with a fluid

simulation. A method to estimate compute the anisotropic capillary pressure for templates

with line and space patterns is presented.

2.2.1 SIMULATION OF RESIST SPREADING IN UVNIL

       When a template contains regularly ordered structures, the droplet located between

the substrate and the template will conform to the structures. The simulation aims are to

elucidate the effects of geometrical structures, particularly a line and space pattern, and

describe the dynamics of fluid flow in a thin gap. Fig. 2.2 is a schematic representation of

a resist droplet with a radius R in a gap height H between a patterned template and a

substrate at a random instant during the spreading of a droplet in UV NIL process. The

liquid-air interface of the droplet results in a pressure gradient that propels the spreading

and causes an attractive capillary force on the template. The ﬂuid ﬂow in the gap results

in a viscous force that balances a capillary force and any externally applied force. Gravity

is neglected in this simulation. It is a reasonable assumption for small drops. The

interfacial tension γ of the gas-liquid interface is assumed constant. The interface has two

different contact angles, θ1 and θ2, made by the imprint material with the template and the

substrate respectively. These two contact angles are also assumed to be constant due to

small capillary number [12].

2.3.3 EFFECT OF FEATURE SIZE

        The general effect of anisotropic capillary pressure arose from the line and space

template structure was observed in the previous section.            In this section, the size

dependency of the capillary pressure boundary condition will be discussed. The height and

width of the feature have a significant influence on the capillary pressure. The larger the

feature height, the size of top area increase (Fig. 2.6). Thus, the larger feature height exerts

a stronger capillary force because the effect of top area get larger as the feature height

increases. The width dependency arose from Equation 2.14, which calculates the capillary

pressure at the top region. Since smaller width results smaller curvature, a larger capillary

pressure is generated (Fig. 2.6). Thus, the smaller width exerts a stronger capillary force

which stretches droplets further in y-direction.

        The effect of feature height appears when comparing the filling time and the shape

result from different capillary pressure. Fig. 2.10 shows the different shapes of droplets

resulted from changing the size of feature height. The feature width is fixed at 0.05. As

expected, the template with higher feature height was elongated more. The simulation with
the higher feature height, h = 0.1 , formed unfilled channels. The simulation with the

smaller feature height, h = 0.025 , has a shape close to a circle (Fig. 2.10). The different

spreading pattern also effect the final filling time. Because of a significant elongation, the
template with feature height, h = 0.1 , merged earlier than the template with feature

height, h = 0.025 . The initial delay caused by the earlier merge propagates until the end

of the simulation, and the higher feature height case takes a longer time to achieve a

complete filling. This observation suggests that it is advantageous to delay the merge as

        These analysis provide two clear guidance in arrangement of droplets. First and

the most important rule of thumb is that it better to merge closer to the end of the filling

process. Second, it is better to reduce the number of merging events. Furthermore, these

analysis suggest alternative method find optimal droplet arrangement. If the template

structure is complex, it is impractical to find effective permeabilities and capillary pressure.

In such case, velocities in x-direction and y-direction or the ratio of the two velocities
should be found experimentally. After finding such quantities, it is possible to find the

optimal droplet arrangement based on two rules: delay droplet merging as much as possible

and reduce the number of merging steps.

2.4 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

        A multi-drop simulation of fluid filling of UVNIL has been further furnished. The

foundation of the simulation is lubrication theory and Young-Laplace equation, and the

volume of fluid method is used to track the merging fluid interface. Anisotropic boundary
                                              53
conditions that accounts for anisotropic flow caused by the line and space template

structure was implemented.

       The key finding of this work is that it is necessary to include both anisotropic

permeability and anisotropic capillary pressure boundary condition to fully describe the

anisotropic fluid flow under a patterned template. This is important in the context of

previous work [9, 10] which could not fully captured the sharp gradient shape of fluid flow

we observed in experiments. The observation made with SE simulation provided the key

ingredient to extrapolate the anisotropic pressure boundary condition used in this work.

For line and space patterns, the droplet spread faster in the direction parallel to the patterns

due to higher permeability and pressure gradient in the direction parallel to the patterns.

       Also, the straight channel formed in the square arrangement and the faster filling

time in the hexagonal arrangement agree with the experimental results [7, 18]. This

agreement between the simulation result and the experimental results proves accuracy of

the simulation approach. The same approach can be applied to various shapes of patterns.

One can describe the change in a flow behavior caused by a large number of features as

averaged permeabilities and study the interface created by features to describe the

anisotropic boundary conditions. This new simulation approach provided two additional

guidance to the several fluid management issues critical to enhancing the speed of the

imprinting process. It has been known that the imprint time decrease with the use if

increasing number or droplets [8, 10]. Addition to the number of droplet, it has been shown

that imprint time decreases with delayed merging moments and decreasing the number of

merging throughout UVNIL process. Finally, if a template structure is highly complicated,

it has been suggested to find both velocities in x-direction and y-direction or the ratio of

the two velocities experimentally to determine the optimal droplet arrangement based on

the two guidance.

3.1 INTRODUCTION

        While research in nanopatterning explores breadth of patterning techniques, there

are currently only two technologies considered for manufacturing of next generation

integrated circuits: Ultraviolet nanoimprint lithography (UVNIL) and extreme ultraviolet

lithography(EUV) [1]. UVNIL is a promising high-resolution molding process. UVNIL

exceeds the patterning limits of 193 nm lithography, the current dominant technology [1].

UVNIL has high resolution and the resolution is largely unaffected by the field size being

patterned [2, 3]. UVNIL is distinct from other lithography processes as it avoids the use

of costly optical systems for traditional photolithography and uses inkjet to dispense resists

material. When compared to an immersion based Self Aligned Quadruple Patterning

(SAQP), cost of ownership for UVNIL is approximately 40% lower [4]. The use of inkjet

allows to print complex patterns with pattern density variations, because inkjet can

distribute resist material according to a non-uniform pattern in the template [1]. Because

the resist volume deposited is matched to the mask pattern density, there is no wasted resist

[5]. The ability of UVNIL to cheaply and reliably replicate nanoscale features is degraded

by low throughput and defect caused by gas trapping. About 70% of throughput budget is

taken by fluid filling step (Fig. 3.1). In order to be a competitive manufacturing process,

UVNIL should reduce the time it required to complete the fluid filling step [1]. It is

possible to increase the throughput by shortening the filling step. This may increase the

number for wafer produced per hour, but at the same time, defects in imprinted pattern

       A typical process of UVNIL is shown in Fig. 3.1. An inkjet dispenser is used to

dispense droplets of low viscosity photo-curable imprint material on the substrate. These

droplets are a few picoliters in volume. A UV-transparent template with micro- or nano-

features is lowered on the substrate. As the template is pressed into resists, the droplets

merge together and fill the gap to form a uniform film. When the desired gap is reached,

UV light irradiates the imprinted film, which photo-polymerizes to form a solid resists

layer with features replicated from the template. The template is then peeled from the resist

with a controlled mechanism completing the patterning process. This pattern can then be

used for further processing such as a mask for reactive ion etch. UV nanoimprint

lithography effectively transfers high-resolution micro- and nano-features from templates

onto substrates and deployed in hard disk industry and photonics applications [1, 4].

       However, process development of UV NIL has been slow. In order to monitor the

filling process and find the exact filling time, it is necessary to perform the same experiment

several times or have real-time observation [6]. The complexity and the large number of

parameters involved with UV NIL makes designing and conducting experiments expensive

[7]. Metrology and inspection techniques are being developed, but significant challenges

still remain for real-time monitoring of UV NIL processes having nanoscale features.

Modeling and simulation tools are helpful for understanding the fundamentals of the filling

steps the UV NIL process [6, 8-10].

 Figure 3.1: Schematic of the main steps for pattern replication in UVNIL and

       There are several studies which tried to optimize the fluid filling step, but mostly

in respect of a uniform patterned template [11-13]. In practice, the nanopatterning template
is composed of regions with different structures and densities. Thus, studies with uniform

patterns may not give a full practical insight which can be used in the manufacturing

process. This complex structure will generate complicated fluid flow behavior which

obscure the optimal arrangement to meet the tight throughput budget. In order to mimic

the complexity of template, two different template structure was combined and the

spreading and merging of droplets were simulated using previously developed simulation

                                            62
base [14]. In this study, the advantage inkjet dispenser is fully explored by dispensing

resist droplets in various arrangement and volume. The objective is to find a practical

arrangement which can be used for template with two different structures. Furthermore,

we present the general approach that can be taken when the optimal droplet arrangement is

not obvious for a specific template.

3.2 SIMULATION METHOD

       A commercially viable nanopatterning process involves a template with arbitrary

pattern complexity and non-uniform pattern density. Optimizing the locations and sizes of

droplets is the key to reducing defects and increasing throughput of UVNIL. However,

optimizing the UVNIL process with a complex template is complicate because there are

multiple factors need to be considered. This study aims to find optimal droplet placement

to meet the limited throughput budget using simulation models describing the flow and

coalescence of the multiple fluid drops.

3.2.2 SIMULATION OF RESIST SPREADING IN VARIOUS CONFIGURATIONS

3.4 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

       The fluid filling of UVNIL under various template structure has been simulated.

The foundation of the simulation is lubrication theory and Young-Laplace equation, and

the volume of fluid method is used to track the merging fluid interface. The key finding

of this work is that it is necessary to distribute the resist in the form of droplets according

to the volume required to fill each template structure. A template is composed of various

regions with different structures. The volume and arrangement of droplets underneath such

complex template strongly affect the imprint time. This study demonstrated the advantage

of inkjet system used in UVNIL. By distributing the correct amount of resist underneath

the template, one can remove the unnecessary fluid transferring step in droplet spreading

and reduce the total filling time.

       The capillary pressure and permeability propel anisotropic flow in UVNIL process

arise from the template structure [14]. Thus, when two or structures are mix in a template,

different fluid flow patterns arise from a single template. In this study, three different

template patterns were studied: flat and line and space, orthogonal line and space, and line

and space with different feature sizes. The mix of structures in templates causes regionally

varied fluid flow. For flat region, the droplet spread isotopically, and for line and space

region, the droplet spread faster in the direction parallel to the patterns due to higher

permeability and pressure gradient in the direction parallel to the patterns. In order to deal

with multiple fluid flow patterns, the droplet placement was regionally optimized. The

optimized droplet arrangement further reduced the total filling time, and the importance of

droplet arrangements was demonstrated. However, the regional optimization strategy

cannot be applied because the optimal arrangement is not known or because of constraints.

In this study, the regional optimization cannot be applied to a template with two different

features size because of droplet number constraints. When comparing the hexagonal
                                              84
arrangement with square arrangement, the filling time of hexagonal arrangement was only

a half of the square arrangement. This improvement in filling time is caused by the

versatility of hexagonal arrangement. The square arrangement can only handle isotropic

cases, but the hexagonal arrangement can handle both isotropic and anisotropic templates.

       In practice, the regional optimization cannot be applied because the optimal

placement is not known. In such case, the hexagonal arrangement is a good starting point,

because the hexagonal arrangement is versatile and possess several advantages. The

hexagonal arrangement can handle both isotropic and anisotropic templates. Furthermore,

the single hexagonal arrangement can handle both horizontal and vertical line and space

template simultaneously, shown in orthogonal line and space pattern case. Combined with

volume distribution and the hexagonal arrangement, starting ground for optimization of

UVNIL process.

        Chapter 4: Marangoni Induced Healing of Capillary Films

4.1 INTRODUCTION

       The dynamics of thin viscous liquid films are ubiquitous in wide variety of natural

phenomena and technological applications. For example, in our daily life, the blink of an

eye involves motion over a thin corneal fluid film [1], drying of paints [2] contact-lens

manufacture [3], and spin coating [4, 5]. In many applications thin films offer advantages

over thicker films apart from the inherent advantages of using less material [6, 7].

However, it is difficult to produce thinner films that are uniform, continuous, and defect-

free film, because thinner films are more susceptible to defects resulting from the coating

process, substrate nonuniformities, or disjoining pressure [6, 7]. Thus, removal of holes

and dry spots generated by defects and disturbances is a challenging engineering problem.

Especially in lithography industry, an ultra-thin lithographic resist film, which have film

thickness of tens of nanometers scale, are quite susceptible to small defects and bubbles

generated during manufacturing process. The fluid dynamics of these thin films is an

interesting both scientific and engineering problem. Stange et al. captured the growth

progression of a small indentation in a 22 nm thick polystyrene film using scanning probe

microscopy [6]. At the nanoscale the dynamics of liquid films are driven by short- and
long-ranged interfacial forces. There is competition between capillary pressure that levels

any perturbation of the film [1], and the disjoining pressure which can amplify the

perturbation [8]. When the short-range disjoining pressure dominates, a hole will grow

and film dewetting will occur. Alternatively, when capillary pressure dominates, the film

will heal the hole by inward spreading driven by surface tension [8, 9]. Thus, the dynamics

of thin liquid films are also an interesting object of study, because describing the thin films

involves understanding interplay between capillarity, hydrodynamics, interfacial transport

                                              88
phenomena, as well as the effects of interaction forces when films thin down to molecular

thicknesses.

        Although the dynamics of dewetting and healing of films have been extensively

studied[6, 9], an important and unstudied case is the effect of Marangoni forces. The

Marangoni effect is fluid flow from regions of lower surface tension to regions of higher

surface tension. The variations in surface tension can come about from variations in

surface temperature (thermocapillary flow) or surface concentration. By adding surfactants

to the thin film, the surface energy of the film decrease. If a dry spot is generated by defects

or bubble, a large concentration gradient between the film and dry spot is generated. The

surface tension gradients give rise to Marangoni forces, which promote the inward flow

and healing of the hole. The influence of the Marangoni forces on the healing process is

the subject of this work.

        In order to study the influence of the Marangoni effect, a film is modelled using the

lubrication approximation, which results in a fourth-order nonlinear partial differential

equation. The contact-line region is described by including a precursor film and a two-

term disjoining pressure.     In order to incorporate the Marangoni force, the species

concentration was tracked by the second order convection-diffusion equation which is

coupled to the thin film equation. Using the theoretical model, the maximum radius of a

hole that can be healed by the surface-tension driven leveling dynamics of a thin viscous

film was studied first. The Marangoni forces were added after, and the effect of the

Marangoni forces on the maximum radius found in the previous case studied. Finally, the

major findings and trends were discussed.

4.4 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

       In this study, the first step was developing a simple energy model to find whether

a hole generated from an external environment, such as, the size of particles, surface

defects, trapped air bubbles, or the energy of external disturbances would grow or not.

From the energy model, it was possible to divide the energy map into two region by finding

the local maxima (Fig 4.6). To confirm the result, a dynamics model was developed. The

foundations of dynamics model were two coupled governing equations, lubrication and

convection-diffusion equations. The energy model was compared with the dynamics

model and the two model agreed well. From the two model, it was found that the smaller

the contact angle, the more stable the film. For example, if contact angle is 2.5 degree, the

film can heal a hole that is about 25 times larger than this thickness. Thus, using a material

that has a lower contact angle is advantageous in generating a stable think film. Also, in



                                             109
this study, the healing time has a strong relationship with the hole radius (Fig 4.7). Smaller

the hole radius fills faster the filling time and the relationship had a reciprocal form.

       The second step was find the effect of Marangoni forces and whether dewetting

cases can be turned into a healing cases under the influence of Marangoni effects. The key

finding of this work is that if the concentration gradient remained until the film touch the

origin, the Marangoni forces promote inward flow and turn dewetting cases into healing

cases. Thus, by adding surfactant, a hole size that larger than the critical size (Fig 4.9) can

be filled. This means that large size defects and bubbles that would original cause films to

dewet can be healed by adding surfactant. In general, a stronger Marangoni forces were

necessary to fill a larger hole, and the critical Marangoni number had a linear relationship

with hole radius size (Fig 4.11).

       Finally, the effect of Péclet number on the critical Marangoni number. The

practical case in mind was change in diffusivity of surfactant. From this study, it was found

the smaller the Péclet number stronger Marangoni forces were necessary, so higher the

critical Marangoni numbers (Fig 4.12). This suggests that if is more advantageous to use

less diffusive surfactant which will maintain the concentration gradient longer thus

Marangoni effects remains longer and promotes healing.

5.1 UVNIL SIMULATION

       Template fluid filling is known to be the bottleneck in ultraviolet nanoimprint

lithography (UVNIL). Insufficient filling time leads to incomplete filling and gas bubbles

which become defects after the curing process. Excessive filling time may reduce defects

but reduces throughput. Optimizing the locations and sizes of droplets is the key to

reducing defects and increasing throughput of UVNIL. The optimization UVNIL process

was the main theme of this work, and computational study of the physics of the fluid

behavior during the imprint process was carried out. The spreading and merging of resist

droplets in UVNIL were simulated by using lubrication theory to describe pressure and

velocity field and Volume of Fluid method to track fluid interfaces. The pressure field is

solved using a parallel implementation of GMRES to reduce the computation time and the

velocity field was found by differentiating the pressure field. The capillary pressure at the

fluid-air interface was used as a boundary condition, thus an interface reconstruction

algorithms were used to define the droplet interface based on fraction of the filling in a

discretized cell. Combined all, the interplay of viscous and capillary forces can be modeled

over the course of the imprint. From previous simulation studies, it was found that more
fluid-air interface increases capillary force which promotes spreading [1, 2]. Thus, it is

favorable to have a larger number of droplets because it increases the capillary force.

       Since the capillary pressure has an important role in filling step of UVNIL, accurate

description of capillary pressure arose by template structure was necessary. The liquid

meniscus formed between a line and space template and a substrate was studied first. The

Concus-Fin condition predicts an endlessly growing filament formation when [3]

       The effect of template structure manifests itself in droplet spreading and overall

filling time. There are two major physical phenomena arose from the structure of a

template: capillary pressure and permeability.         In the line and space template, both

anisotropic capillary pressure and permeabilites propel anisotropic flow in filling process.

In general, the permeabilities are scale as O(h2) initially, but as the gap gets thinner, the

permeability becomes significantly higher in the direction parallel to the line and space

pattern compared to the orthogonal direction. Also, the pressure gradient is larger at the

tip of the droplet than the side of the droplet because of the anisotropic capillary pressure
boundary condition. The combination of the two effect causes droplets to spread faster in

the direction parallel to the line and space pattern and elongates the droplet shape along the

parallel direction. The droplet merges in the parallel direction first, and the early merging

caused by anisotropic pressure boundary condition and permeability impeded the filing

speed and increased the overall filling time compared to the plate template case.

       The feature size dependency of the capillary pressure boundary condition was also

studied. The larger feature height exerts a stronger capillary force because the area and the
                                             113
effect of template features, which have a smaller curvature, get larger as the feature height

increases. There is width dependency; smaller the width a stronger capillary force arises.

A smaller width results smaller curvature which generates a larger capillary pressure.

Thus, a higher feature height and a smaller feature width exert a stronger capillary force

which stretches droplets further in the parallel direction.

5.2 EFFECT OF DROPLET ARRANGEMENTS AND VOLUME

       Unlike isotropic flow, where droplets spread equally in all direction, in anisotropic

flow droplets spread faster in one direction than the other. Thus, if droplets are arranged

in a square fashion under a vertical line and space template, droplets merge first in the

vertical direction and become larger droplets. The lack of capillary pressure at the interface

impedes the spreading of the larger droplets.         This observation suggests that it is

advantageous to delay the merge as long as possible and an optimal way to arrange the

drop. As a rule of thumb, it is better to reduce the number of merging instances and delay

the merging instance as much as possible. Since the flow velocity is proportional to the

capillary pressure at the interface until droplets merge, the ratio between the parallel

direction and the orthogonal direction traveled by a droplet was estimated. The estimated

ratio remained close to two throughout the spreading. An optimal arrangement was found

to be a hexagonal arrangement, where the vertical distance between droplets are two times

larger than the horizontal distance. Unlike the square arrangement which had two merging

events, the hexagonal arrangement merged simultaneously into a single drop at the end,

and it was found that the hexagonal arrangement was more than two times faster than the

square arrangement.

       The general rule of thumb can be applied to a more complex template, where

patterns are no longer uniform. Three different cases were studied: (1) a template

                                             114
composed of a flat pattern and a horizontal line and space pattern, (2) a template composed

of a horizontal line and space pattern and a vertical line and space pattern, and (3) a

template composed of two vertical line and space patterns, which has different features

sizes. From these simulations, it is found that the volume and arrangement of droplets

underneath strongly affect the imprint time. By distributing the correct amount of resist

underneath the template, one can remove the unnecessary fluid transferring step in droplet

spreading and reduce the total filling time. Furthermore, by optimally placing the resist

droplets, one can delay merging events and accelerate the spreading speed. Finally, the

advantage of hexagonal arrangements are explored.

5.3 SIMULATION OF THIN FILM FLOW

       The dynamics of thin viscous liquid films are ubiquitous in wide variety of natural

phenomena and technological application. The understanding of the viscous flow and

stability of these films is an interesting scientific and engineering problem because there

are multiple physics involved in describing such films and used in many manufacturing

processes. In lithography industry, for example, ultra-thin polymer coatings are frequently

used in e.g., nanolithography [5] and nanoimprinting [6] as well as in the development of

non-volatile computer memory devices [7]. However, it is difficult to produce thinner

films that are uniform, continuous, and defect-free film, because thinner films are more

susceptible to defects. Thus, in many cases, manufacturing problems involve liquid films

interested in removal of holes of dry spots generated by outside disturbances.

       The dewetting and healing of thin films by using lubrication equations and

convection diffusion equations. Two governing equations are coupled, so they had to be

solved simultaneously. The coupled equations are solved using a semi-implicit method

due to severe stability requirement. The axisymmetric geometry of a hole in a thin-film

                                           115
was exploited and the flow was one-dimensional in radial direction. Also, symmetry

boundary condition was applied at the origin of radial coordinate. It was assumed that

there is an infinite reservoir of fluid outside of hole. By tracking the species concentration

using convention-diffusion equation, the Marangoni effect was incorporated into the

simulations. The incorporation of Marangoni forces distinguish this study from previous

studies which studied the dynamics of hole healing and dewetting.
