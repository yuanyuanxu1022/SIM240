Rectangular-groove theories closer to the present geometry are also available. Ng [12] used domain decomposition and eigenfunction matching to solve no-slip, pressure-driven Stokes flow between two rectangular-grooved walls, considering both principal directions and pattern phase shifts. Both walls are grooved, unlike the present flat-wall/grooved-wall configuration. Moreover, the longitudinal effective slip is defined using only the clear-layer flux between the wall envelopes, excluding cavity flux; it cannot directly replace the present conductance integrated over all fluid. For a flat wall opposing a sinusoidally modulated wall, Stroock et al. [13] used a small-amplitude expansion to analyze directional fluxes and anisotropic effective boundary conditions, showing how oblique grooves generate transverse flow. Directional response and reconstructed flux deflection therefore do not, by themselves, establish novelty here.

For pressure losses, Mohammadi and Floryan [14] reported groove-orientation effects and distinguished contributions from changes in mean wall position and geometry-induced flow modulation. Our citation is restricted to the publisher abstract; unverified full-text coefficients are not adopted or ranked for accuracy. Their loss partition should not be identified with our gradient-energy grouping relative to a local-plate baseline. The checked pressure-driven examples of Tran et al. [15] use local slip conditions, whereas their explicit no-slip corrugated-wall example is a sinusoidal shear flow. Neither supplies a matched reference for the present pressure-driven rectangular grooves with no slip on all walls. These studies delimit our assessment of local-cubic-law discrepancies and their anisotropy consequences within a specified rectangular family, supplemented by a gradient-structure diagnostic on a separate set. Differences in geometry or grouping alone establish neither priority nor a universal mechanism.
