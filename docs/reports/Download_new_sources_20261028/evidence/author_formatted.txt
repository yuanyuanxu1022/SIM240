   Flow through microchannels with topographically
 patterned walls: a spectral theory for arbitrary groove
                         depths
                 Mainendra Kumar Dewangan, Subhra Datta
Department of Mechanical Engineering, IIT Delhi, Hauz Khas, New Delhi 110016, India




Abstract
Current technology allows us to build microfluidic channels, which are so
thin that incidental and/or surface topographical features engineered for
applications such as microfluidic mixing/separations can present significant
constrictions and dilatations to the flow passage. Considering such situa-
tions, pressure-driven laminar incompressible flow through an infinitely wide
channel, one of whose walls has sinusoidal grooves running along the flow
direction, is investigated through an accurate semi-analytical and grid-free
spectral approach, without placing any restrictions on the pattern amplitude.
In the special limits of small pattern amplitude and large channel spacing,
the spectral model recovers asymptotic predictions earlier reported in the
literature. The spectral model is also used to provide new closed-form esti-
mates for permeability and effective slip at an order of asymptotic accuracy
higher than those currently available from the literature. The predictions
from the spectral model, which are obtainable with very low computational
expense, are also cross-validated against finite-element-method based simu-
lations. In the thick channel limit, the predictions from the spectral model is
quantitatively consistent with results from recent molecular dynamic (MD)
simulations conducted by Guo et al. [1, Physical Review Fluids, 1(7), 074102,
2016]. The newly proposed and existing closed-form asymptotic predictions
are also assessed numerically against the spectral model. In the long-wave
limit, the permeability of the channel increases on increasing the depth of
grooves, while in the short wave limit, the permeability decreases. Certain

1. Introduction
    Topographically patterned surfaces have numerous practical applications
to microfluidics, such as microfluidic mixing [2], pumping and flow detection
[3], control of dispersion of analytes [4] and surface capture of bioparticles
such as circulating tumor cells [5, 6]. In many such applications, fluid motion
near the patterned surface play a critical role. With continued progress in
miniaturization from microfluidics to nanofluidics [7], it is now quite proba-
ble that the passage for microfluidic flows will be obstructed by either engi-
neered topographic patterns or unintentional irregularities in fabricated sur-
faces such as waviness and roughness. Therefore, theoretical understanding
of situations in confined flows where one or more channel walls are topo-
graphically patterned and the amplitude and wavelength of the patterning is
comparable to the size of the channels is necessary.
    There are three main scales in a confined forced flow problem with surface
topographic patterns: the channel size, the pattern amplitude and pattern
pitch. Early theoretical approaches on pressure-driven/electrokinetic con-
fined flow problems often use approximations based on relative magnitude of
these three scales. The assumption of the depth of patterned grooves being
much smaller than the channel size is used by [8, 9]. The studies by [10–12]
employ the asymptotic approximation that the amplitude of the pattern is
much smaller than the pattern pitch. Apart from [8, 9], earlier work have
ignored the confinement effects by using an analytical simplification corre-
sponding to one of the confining surface to be far enough to merely provide
an ‘outer flow’, while the inner flow can be solved with a prescribed shear
at infinity [10–18]. Such a flow field is termed ‘shear flow’. However, it can
be noted that [11, 12, 17, 18] allow for intrinsic wall slip, which introduces
another length scale to the problem.
    From the standpoint of applications such as microfluidic mixing discussed
above, grooves obliquely oriented to the flow are important. Crucially, flow
fields altered by oblique groove patterns, can be rendered tractable by utiliz-


                                      2
ing the tensorial effective-slip [11] and effective permeability [3] formalisms,
with the latter more appropriate for confined flows. These tensorial ap-
proaches reveal that, to represent confined/unconfined flow over oblique pat-
terns, it is sufficient to solve for the flow field along the two principal di-
rections, viz. along and across the grooves of the pattern. The works of
[13–15, 17, 18] on shear flows (without further asymptotic approximations)
furnish flow fields across the pattern, whereas the other studies referenced
above [8–12] explore both flows along and across the pattern through asymp-
totic approaches.
    Studies on flows with patterned topographies may also be classified based
on the geometrical shape of the patterns studied. Richardson [13] studies
a one-parameter family of surfaces that facilitate his conformal map, but
does not allow for independent adjustment of the pitch and amplitude of
the patterns. Sinusoidal surface topographies characterized by an amplitude
and wavelength (pitch) are studied by [8–10, 12, 14, 17, 18], with the crucial
formalism for synthesis of flow transverse to more complex patterns being
established through Fourier analysis from sinusoids established from the in
[17]. Rectangular grooves, which are easy to fabricate, have been modelled
by [10, 14, 19]. It can be noted that [10, 14] study both sinusoidal and
rectangular grooves.
    Methodologies employed for modelling the local and effective properties
of flow in channels with patterned walls wetted by the liquid include experi-
mental [9, 20] approaches; molecular dynamics simulations [1]; finite-element
[1, 21], point collocation method [10, 19], finite-volume method [22–24], fi-
nite difference method [25] and Lattice-Boltzmann method [26]. In situations
where patterns are not necessarily wetted by the liquid leading to the Cassie
[27] rather than the Wenzel state relevant to the current study, dissipative
particle dynamics [28], phase-field method combined with a stochastic adap-
tation to the Navier-Stokes system [29], and a spectral approach [30, 31] have
also been used. There are physically distinct applications of surface topogra-
phies outside the domain of microfluidics, such as in the study of turbulent
boundary layers [32], which are excluded from consideration here, wherein
the flows cannot be modelled as steady, laminar and possessing zero inertia,
as in all the works referred to above.
    In this study, a confined flow along a sinusoidal pattern of wall topogra-
phy is modelled. Taking advantage of the periodic nature of the flow field,
a grid free spectral approach is employed. Grid-based approaches that use
algebraic interpolation (or Taylor series approximations) such as finite differ-

                                       3
ence, finite volume, finite element and point collocation methods are subject
to truncation errors that decay algebraically on grid refinement. Unlike these
methods, the spectral model is subject to exponentially fast decay of trunca-
tion errors on progressive addition of modes; further there is no aliasing error
due to absence of a grid [33]. Under the situations studied, the computa-
tional expense of the spectral method were found to be negligible compared
to finite element method which was used for cross-validation. In addition, a
crucial advantage of a spectral approach to the current problem and a key
theme of the current study is analytical reductibility to closed form results
in special limits.
    The pattern amplitude can be as large as physically admissible, thus
allowing establishment of solid-solid contact between opposing walls. In ad-
dition, the model places no restrictions on the relative magnitudes of the
pattern pitch and the channel height. One wall of the channel is patterned,
whereas the other wall is flat. This flow configuration is similar to [9], thereby
facilitating assessment of the numerical significance of the small-amplitude
restriction in [9]. In practice too, the requirements of optical access to the
microchannel and correct alignment between walls can necessitate at least
one wall of a microchannel to be flat [34]. Closed form results from the liter-
ature in the shear-flow (or thick channel) limit and small amplitude limit are
shown to arise as special cases. The spectral method also offers a framework
to extend these approximations to higher orders of asymptotic accuracy, as
demonstrated with the small amplitude approximation.
    Confinement effects are emphasized when the channel height and the
pattern amplitude become comparable. The spectral model is used to show
that the presence of confinement effects cause the hydraulic permeability of
the channel to behave in a qualitatively and quantitatively different manner
than in several analytically accessible situations studied in the literature
[9, 10, 12, 17, 18] where patterns merely act as roughness elements.
    In Section 2, the spectral approach is discussed in detail, and useful an-
alytically tractable limits are derived, which either corroborate or extend
existing asymptotic analyses from the literature. Predictions from the spec-
tral model, corroborated against finite element simulations and contrasted
against asymptotic predictions are discussed in Section 3. Formal justifica-
tion for certain asymptotic estimates made in this section are provided in the
Appendices. In Section 4, the significant conclusions from the current study
and the scope for future work are outlined. Methodological aspects of the
spectral and finite element simulations are discussed in the Appendices.

totic theories discussed above to small pattern amplitudes. It is therefore
important to numerically assess if this is true.

    The observed trends in Fig. 2 and the above discussion of effects of
pattern amplitude and wavelength can be rationalized as follows. Rougher
the patterns (or larger the value of α), larger is the additional area on the
lower wall (Fig. 1) over which viscous forces can act to enforce no slip, in
comparison to a channel with a plane lower wall at Y=0. For short pattern
wavelengths, the most significant gradients in the flow are those near the
lower wall, which scale inversely with the wavelength, accentuating the role of
the viscous forces acting on the lower surface. This effect causes permeability
to decrease with α at short pattern wavelengths. Thus, for short waves, the
grooves act as drag enhancing roughness elements.
    In the long-wave limit, the x-gradients in the flow, which are controlled by
the wave-length become much less significant than the y-gradients controlled
by the local channel thickness. As a result, the channel acts like a Hele-
Shaw cell [34, 38–42], where the overall hydraulic permeability is controlled
by the the permeability of the dilated parts of the flow cross-section. This
causes the permeability to increase monotonically with α at large enough
wavelengths, as increasing α increases the size of the dilatations. For long
waves, the above-discussed confinement effect becomes dominant.
    At moderate wavelengths, initially at small values of α, grooves act as
friction enhancing roughness elements. However, as α− is increased, a tran-
sition toward a long-wave like positive slope in the permeability versus α
curve occurs, since the progressively narrower constrictions created on in-
creasing α enhance the relative significance of the y−gradients in compari-
son to x−gradients. As discussed above, this transition is possible only when
1.92 < λ < 3.28.

rent study to other periodic corrugation shapes, as suggested for shear flow
across corrugations by [17]. In fact, Eq. (5) is equally valid for any periodic
corrugation shape, although with a different set of values for the constants
Cn . However, if the corrugation is non-smooth, as in the case of easily realiz-
able rectangular/square grooves, the exponentially fast decay of the spectral
method with the number of terms may be lost [33]. Rectangular (or ‘par-

with Eq. (5), as demonstrated by [12] for shear flow along corrugations.
Qualitatively correct predictions are possible for square grooves by tightly
inscribing a sinusoidal shape, as shown in the experimental comparisons of
Ref. [9]. Consideration of viscous effects of the discontinuities in the surface
profile and scaling consequences of the two flat zones occurring per pattern
period qualitatively suggest that, with rectangular grooves, the short-wave-
limit decay of permeability as well as the long-wave-limit enhancement of
permeability on increasing the groove depth may be stronger effects than
observed with sinusoidal grooves.

4. Conclusions
    The flow through an infinitely wide channel, one of whose walls has sinu-
soidal grooves/corrugations running along the direction of an applied pressure
differential is studied using a semi-analytical and grid-free spectral approach,
without restricting the corrugation amplitude. The corrugation amplitude is
allowed to be large enough to alter significantly the shape of the cross-section
of the flow directed along the corrugations, and can even create contact with
the planar surface forming the other channel wall. The spectral predictions
on hydraulic permeability, which are cross-validated against finite element
simulations, show qualitative characteristics not obtainable from asymptotic
predictions available in the literature [9–11]. For example, the spectral ap-
proach reveals that there is an intermediate range of wavelengths, when the

                                      20
permeability can first decrease with increasing amplitude and then increase,
leading to the appearance of a mimimum. The asymptotic prediction of
Ref. [9], which cannot resolve the above minima shows monotonic decrease
of permeability for small wavelengths and increase of permeability for large
wavelengths, with a somewhat un-physical appearance of a critical wave-
length at which the permeability is not sensitive to amplitude. Similarly, as
the spacing between the corrugated and flat plates forming the channel is
decreased a minimum appears in the permeability of the patterned channel
scaled relative to that of an unpatterned channel with same pitch-averaged
spacing between the walls. Dimensional estimates of the minimum corru-
gation amplitude and minimum height, as relevant to typical microfluidic
configurations have been provided in Section 3.
    The closed-form expressions for permeability appearing in [9] and effective
slip length appearing in [10] are shown to correspond to be formal asymp-
totic approximations of the spectral predictions, where only one mode in the
spectral representation of the flow-field contributes to the permeability. A
new spectrally-derived small-amplitude estimate of permeability (Eq.(14))
which would formally be equivalent to the estimate from domain perturba-
tions technique of Ref. [9], when carried to the next higher asymptotic order,
is also provided. The limit of large channel spacing, which under the scales
chosen becomes equivalent to shear flow is also explored and a new asymp-
totic prediction is proposed, which allows a closed-form representation of
slip, that is more accurate for larger amplitude patterns than the predictions
available from Refs. [10, 11, 15].
    It can be noted that the asymptotic results of Ref. [9] has been refer-
enced widely to conceptualize, design, rationalize and validate performance
of microfluidic devices such as mixers [4–6, 44]. However, the asymptotic pre-
dictions need assessment against numerical/exact solutions to ascertain their
usefulness in terms of how small the relevant perturbation parameters should
be in order for the corresponding theory to be useful in a practically relevant
input parameter space [36]. In this study, several numerical assessments of
asymptotic theories have been made.
    The spectral and finite-element predictions accurately match each other
closely, for all situations studied. However, asymptotic predictions differ sig-
nificantly from spectral predictions, even under situations where they show
same qualitative trends. The accuracy of asymptotic prediction of Ref. [9]
(or the spectrally derived Eq. (13)) degrades significantly with increase in
the amplitude. Especially at small wavelengths, the corresponding relative

                                      21
departure from spectral/finite-element results are quantitatively unaccept-
able (e.g. 20% relative error, even at a fairly ’small’ amplitude equaling 20%
of channel width). At small wavelengths, the asymptotic prediction of Ref.
[9] overestimates the frictional resistance of the channel, whereas for large
wavelengths the frictional resistance is underestimated. For shear flow, the
resistance of the patterned surface is over-estimated by the asymptotic effec-
tive slip prediction of Ref. [10], which is formally equivalent to the spectrally
derived Eq. (16). These trends may be useful to consider in interpreting fu-
ture experiments with asymptotic models such as that described in [9]. The
mismatch between asymptotic and spectral results decreases on increasing
the wavelength of the pattern. Higher order asymptotic predictions derived
in the current study improve the numerical accuracy for moderately long and
long wavelengths, but not for short wavelengths.
    Interestingly, recent molecular dynamics results [1] are also found to be
in close agreement with spectral results on shear flow, if particular data
sets from [1] with strong wall fluid interactions and sinusoidally ’bent’ ar-
rangement of atoms are chosen, and a correction inspired by effective slip
predictions in [11, 12] is made for intrinsic slip.
    In the future work, a flow configuration where the flow occurs across
the corrugations may be studied, which, when combined with the current
results, will allow applications to mixing devices by virtue of the tensorial
formalism of Refs. [3, 11]. Also intrinsic slip should be considered to under-
stand to what extent roughness/corrugations compete or collaborate with
slip effects in defining the flow field and permeability. It is anticipated that
that the behaviour of hydraulic permeability explained here will useful on
surface-texture and microchannel design for various applications e.g. passive
mixers, pumps, flow detectors and separation devices for microfluidics [44–
46], enhanced surface capture of bio-particles [6]. Naturally rough surfaces
have more uneven shapes, whereas engineered surfaces commonly have cor-
rugations composed of planar segments. Therefore, generalizing the current
work through Fourier analysis to more general shapes of corrugation than a
sinusoid will be useful.

Appendix C. Additional details on numerical methods
    The finite element simulations were conducted in the commercial finite
element software COMSOL Multiphysics R . Grid independence up to three
decimal places in the predicted dimensionless permeabilities were assured all
over the parameter space explored in Section 3, which required a uniformly
sized triangular elements of size 0.02 times the average channel size. The ma-
trix inversion procedure was performed using the built-in function‘mldivide’
of the software Matlab R . Truncation of the equation system in Eq. (8) to
6 − 15 terms were sufficient to achieve three digit accuracy in permeability
predictions of the spectral method. In obtaining the permeability predictions
for 100 points between α = 0 and α = 1, the spectral solution was about
250-300 times faster than the finite-element-method simulation on a work-
station with 3.40 GHz processor. The root finding required for solving Eq.
(13) when the right hand side is unity, as discussed in Section 3 used the
built in function ’fzero’ of Matlab R .
