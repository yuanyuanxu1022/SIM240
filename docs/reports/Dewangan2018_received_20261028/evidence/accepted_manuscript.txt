Abstract
Current technology allows us to build microfluidic channels, which are so
thin that incidental and/or surface topographical features engineered for
applications such as microfluidic mixing/separations can present significant
constrictions and dilatations to the flow passage. Considering such situa-
tions, pressure-driven laminar incompressible flow through an infinitely wide
channel, one of whose walls has sinusoidal grooves running along the flow
direction, is investigated through an accurate semi-analytical and grid-free
spectral approach, without placing any restrictions on the pattern amplitude.
In the special limits of small pattern amplitude, thick and thin channels , the
spectral model recovers asymptotic predictions earlier reported in the liter-
ature. The spectral model is also used to provide new closed-form estimates
for permeability and effective slip at an order of asymptotic accuracy higher
than those currently available from the literature. The effect of unequal de-
grees of intrinsic hydrodynamic slippage on the corrugated and plane walls is
also studied asymptotically. The predictions from the spectral model, which
are obtainable with very low computational expense, are also cross-validated
against finite-element-method based simulations. In the thick channel limit,
the predictions from the spectral model are quantitatively consistent with
results from recent molecular dynamic (MD) simulations conducted by Guo
et al. [1, Physical Review Fluids, 1(7), 074102, 2016]. The newly proposed
and existing closed-form asymptotic predictions are also assessed numeri-
cally against the spectral model. In the long-wave limit, the permeability of

1. Introduction
    Topographically patterned surfaces have numerous practical applications
to microfluidics, such as microfluidic mixing [2], pumping and flow detection
[3], control of dispersion of analytes [4] and surface capture of bioparticles
such as circulating tumor cells [5, 6]. In many such applications, fluid motion
near the patterned surface play a critical role. With continued progress in
miniaturization from microfluidics to nanofluidics [7], it is now quite proba-
ble that the passage for microfluidic flows will be obstructed by either engi-
neered topographic patterns or unintentional irregularities in fabricated sur-
faces such as waviness and roughness. Therefore, theoretical understanding
of confined flows with surface topographical patterns deserve attention
    Early theoretical approaches on pressure-driven/electrokinetic confined
flow problems often use approximations based on relative magnitude of these
three length scales The assumption of the depth of patterned grooves being
much smaller than the channel size is used by [8, 9]. The studies by [10–12]
employ the asymptotic approximation that the amplitude of the pattern is
much smaller than the pattern pitch. Apart from [8, 9], earlier work have
ignored confinement effects by focusing on shear flows [10–18]. Such a flow
field is termed ‘shear flow’. However, it can be noted that [11, 12, 17, 18]
allow for intrinsic wall slip, which introduces another length scale to the
problem.
    From the standpoint of applications such as microfluidic mixing discussed
above, grooves obliquely oriented to the flow are important. Crucially, flow
fields altered by oblique groove patterns, can be rendered tractable by utiliz-
ing the tensorial effective-slip [11] and effective permeability [3] formalisms,
with the latter more appropriate for confined flows. These tensorial ap-
proaches reveal that, to represent confined/unconfined flow over oblique pat-
terns, it is sufficient to solve for the flow field along the two principal di-

                                       2
rections, viz. along and across the grooves of the pattern. The works of
[13–15, 17, 18] on shear flows furnish flow fields across the pattern, whereas
the other studies referenced above [8–12] explore both flows along and across
the pattern .
    Studies on flows with patterned topographies may also be classified based
on the geometrical shape of the patterns studied. Richardson [13] studies a
one-parameter family of surfaces that facilitate his conformal map, but does
not allow for independent adjustment of the pitch and amplitude of the pat-
terns. Sinusoidal surface topographies characterized by an amplitude and
wavelength are studied by [8–10, 12, 14, 17, 18], with the crucial formalism
for synthesis of flow transverse to more complex patterns being established
through Fourier analysis from sinusoids established from the in [17]. Rectan-
gular grooves, which are easy to fabricate, have been modelled by [10, 14, 19].
It can be noted that [10, 14] study both sinusoidal and rectangular grooves.
    Methodologies employed for modelling the local and effective properties
of flow in channels with patterned walls wetted by the liquid include experi-
mental [9, 20] approaches; molecular dynamics simulations [1]; finite-element
[1, 21], point collocation method [10, 19], finite-volume method [22–24], fi-
nite difference method [25] and Lattice-Boltzmann method [26]. In situations
where patterns are not necessarily wetted by the liquid leading to the Cassie
[27] rather than the Wenzel state relevant to the current study, dissipative
particle dynamics [28], phase-field method combined with a stochastic adap-
tation to the Navier-Stokes system [29], and a spectral approach [30, 31] have
also been used. There are physically distinct applications of surface topogra-
phies outside the domain of microfluidics, such as in the study of turbulent
boundary layers [32], which are excluded from consideration here, wherein
the flows cannot be modelled as steady, laminar and possessing zero inertia,
as in all the works referred to above.
    In this study, a confined flow along a sinusoidal pattern of wall topogra-
phy is modelled. Taking advantage of the periodic nature of the flow field,
a grid free spectral approach is employed. Grid-based approaches that use
algebraic interpolation (or Taylor series approximations) such as finite differ-
ence, finite volume, finite element and point collocation methods are subject
to truncation errors that decay algebraically on grid refinement. Unlike these
methods, the spectral model is subject to exponentially fast decay of trunca-
tion errors on progressive addition of modes; further there is no aliasing error
due to absence of a grid [33]. Under the situations studied, the computa-
tional expense of the spectral method were found to be negligible compared

                                       3
to finite element method which was used for cross-validation. In addition, a
crucial advantage of a spectral approach to the current problem and a key
theme of the current study is analytical reductibility to closed form results
in special limits.
    The pattern amplitude can be as large as physically admissible, thus
allowing establishment of solid-solid contact between opposing walls. In ad-
dition, the model places no restrictions on the relative magnitudes of the
pattern pitch and the channel height. One wall of the channel is patterned,
whereas the other wall is flat. This flow configuration is similar to [9], thereby
facilitating assessment of the numerical significance of the small-amplitude
restriction in [9]. In practice too, the requirements of optical access to the
microchannel and correct alignment between walls can necessitate at least
one wall of a microchannel to be flat [34]. Closed form results from the liter-
ature in the shear-flow (or thick channel) limit and small amplitude limit are
shown to arise as special cases. The spectral method also offers a framework
to extend these approximations to higher orders of asymptotic accuracy, as
demonstrated with the small amplitude approximation.
    In Section 2, the spectral approach is discussed in detail, and useful an-
alytically tractable limits are derived, which either corroborate or extend
existing asymptotic analyses from the literature.New asymptotic predictions
on the effect of hydrodynamic slippage are also presented in this section. Pre-
dictions from the spectral model, corroborated against finite element simula-
tions and contrasted against asymptotic predictions are discussed in Section
3. Formal justification for certain asymptotic estimates made in this section
are provided in the Appendices. In Section 4, the significant conclusions from
the current study and the scope for future work are outlined. Methodologi-
cal aspects of the spectral and finite element simulations are discussed in the
Appendices.

3. Results and discussion
    The asymptotic results from the literature [9, 10] and the new asymptotic
results proposed here are strictly limited to “small” values of their gauge
functions, e.g. α → 0 in Eqs. (13) and (14). Formally, the spectral model
should circumvent this limitation of the asymptotic theories. It is therefore
important to numerically assess if this is true.
    The six panes of Fig. 2 shows the variation of the dimensionless perme-
ability with the dimensionless amplitude α for various values of dimensionless
pattern pitches . Corresponding predictions from finite-element simulations
(labeled as ‘Numerical’ in the legends), obtained using the software Comsol
Multiphysics R , are used to cross-validate the predictions from the spectral
model, as shown on panes (a)-(e). Further details on the finite-element sim-
ulations are available in Appendix D.
    Panes (a)-(e) of Fig. 2 demonstrate excellent agreement between predic-
tions from finite-element and the spectral method. The spectral model was
used to identify distinct short-wave (λ < 1.92), intermediate-wave (1.92 <
λ < 3.28) and long-wave (λ > 3.28) regimes in the behavior of permeability
with the groove depth.
    For shorter wavelengths (λ < 1.92) such as λ = 1 shown in pane (a), the
permeability, decreases monotonically with increasing pattern amplitude. At
intermediate wavelengths in the range 1.92 < λ < 3.28, the permeability
passes through a minimum, as evident from pane (b) for λ = 3 and its inset.
The reason for the appearance of this minimum is discussed later in the
section, after certain other closely related observations are reported.

The spectral predictions reveal that the asymptotic prediction Q ≃ Q0 is
valid with less than 1.1% error up to α < 0.4, although with a three-digit
accurate numerically calculated slope of 0.101 at α = 0.4, in place of the
asymptotically predicted zero slope. For large values of α, the spectral pre-
dictions rise steeply as α is increased, making the asymptotic estimate
Q = Q0 inadequate; e.g at α = 0.9, Q is 14.3% larger than Q0 . However, λ∗

is monotonic increasing (with no minimum).
    The observed trends in Fig. 2 and the above discussion of effects of
pattern amplitude and wavelength can be rationalized as follows. Rougher
the patterns (or larger the value of α), larger is the additional area on the
lower wall (Fig. 1) over which viscous forces can act to enforce no slip, in
comparison to a channel with a plane lower wall at Y=0. For short pattern

                                      15
wavelengths, the most significant gradients in the flow are those near the
lower wall, which scale inversely with the wavelength, accentuating the role of
the viscous forces acting on the lower surface. This effect causes permeability
to decrease with α at short pattern wavelengths. Thus, for short waves, the
grooves act as drag enhancing roughness elements.
    In the long-wave limit, the x-gradients in the flow, which are controlled by
the wave-length become much less significant than the y-gradients controlled
by the local channel thickness. As a result, the channel may be considered
to consist of several constant x lamellae, where viscous forces between the
lamellae are much less significant than viscous forces between elements at
various y levels of the lamellae. Therefore, the lamellae behave like series-
connected hydraulic resistors, where Poiseuille Law applies determines the
scaling 1/(h30 (1 − α)3 ) and 1/(h30 (1 + α)3 ) for the hydraulic resistance of
the thinnest and thickest lamellae at the peak and trough of the bottom
topography. Thus, the equivalent hydralic conductance (or permeability) of
these two lamellae is h30 [(1 − α)3 + (1 + α)3 ∼ h30 [1 + O(α2 )]; this scaling
behavior is found to be consistent with the equivalent conductance of all
lamellae given by Eq. (19) and found by the more rigorous approach of
taking the ‘lubrication limit’ of the spectral theory. Qualitatively, in the long
wave limit, the increased conductance of dilated sectors overcompensate the
reduced conductance of constricted sectors. This causes the permeability
to increase monotonically with α at large enough wavelengths, as increasing
α increases the size of the dilatations. For long waves, the above-discussed
confinement effect becomes dominant.
    At moderate wavelengths, initially at small values of α, grooves act as
friction enhancing roughness elements leading to a negative slope in the per-
meability versus amplitude curve, as seen on Fig. 2b. However, as α is
increased, a transition through a minimum, as seen on the inset of the same
figure, toward a long-wave like positive slope in the permeability versus α
curve occurs, since the progressively narrower constrictions created on in-
creasing α enhance the relative significance of the y−gradients in compari-
son to x−gradients. As discussed above, this transition is possible only when
1.92 < λ < 3.28.

    Some important aspects of the effect of hydrodynamic slippage on the
corrugated and/or the planar walls can be inferred from studying the H de-
pendence of the coefficients of α2 in Eqs.(22) and (23). First, among all
two-dimensional channels of with sinusoidally modulated cross-section con-
structed with one no-slip and one perfect-slip surface having a given average
dimensionless thickness H, if the perfect slip resides on the corrugated sur-
face,a larger flow will obtained than when perfect slip resides on the planar
surface. This is because, for all values of H, Q/Q0 predicted by Eq.(22) ex-
ceeds Q/Q0 predicted by Eq.(23). Another important implication of Eqs.(22)
and (23) is that for sufficiently thick channels, permeability of a channel with
perfect slip on planar walls can decrease with amplitude, in a manner similar
to no-slip channels, for there is a zero of the coefficient of α2 near H ≃ 1.2.
On the other hand, the permeability of a channel with perfect slip on the
corrugated walls is always larger than that of a planar channel (Q > Q0 from
Eq.(22)). The overall qualitative conclusion is that the fluidic resistance is
more sensitive to friction or lack there of on the corrugated wall than on the
planar wall.
    Formally, through Fourier analysis it may be possible to extend the cur-
rent study to other periodic corrugation shapes, as suggested for shear flow
across corrugations by [17]. However, with more general corrugation profiles,
if spectrally accurate results are desired, numerical evaluation of the discrete
Fourier transform may be necessary. On the other hand, if asymptotically

                                       21
accurate results are desired, the procedure shown in Section 2 can be followed
closely. In fact, Eq. (5) is equally valid for any periodic corrugation shape, al-
though with a different set of values for the constants Cn . However, if the cor-
rugation is non-smooth, as in the case of easily realizable rectangular/square
grooves, the exponentially fast decay of the spectral method with the number
of terms may be lost [33]. Rectangular (or ‘parallel’) grooves may also be
analyzable by a matching approach combined with Eq. (5), as demonstrated
by [12] for shear flow along corrugations. Qualitatively correct predictions
are possible for square grooves by tightly inscribing a sinusoidal shape, as
shown in the experimental comparisons of Ref. [9]. Consideration of viscous
effects of the discontinuities in the surface profile and scaling consequences
of the two flat zones occurring per pattern period qualitatively suggest that,
with rectangular grooves, the short-wave-limit decay of permeability as well
as the long-wave-limit enhancement of permeability on increasing the groove
depth may be stronger effects than observed with sinusoidal grooves. It is
difficult to characterize realistic roughness profiles, e.g. those found in rock
fractures, which have fractal characteristics, with a single length scale such
as L used here. However, in rock fractures, as discussed by [39], longer waves
correspond to larger r.m.s. roughness heights (proportional to amplitude a),
and larger the fractal dimension of the rock walls, steeper is the decay of
the r.m.s. roughness with spatial frequency. It can thus be argued that the
longest wave has the most dominant contribution to the hydraulic perme-
ability. Recognizing that rock fractures are typically rather thin compared
to their length, the small  and small H limits of the current study assume
significance, even though the ‘roughness profile’ used here is a sinusoid of
single wavelength. In fact, the small H limit permeability predictions shown
here are independent of the “dominant wavelength”, which may be difficult
to measure [44].

4. Conclusions
    The flow through an infinitely wide channel, one of whose walls has sinu-
soidal grooves/corrugations running along the direction of an applied pressure
differential is studied using a semi-analytical and grid-free spectral approach.
The corrugation amplitude is allowed to be large enough to alter significantly
the shape of the cross-section of the flow directed along the corrugations, and
can even create contact with the planar surface forming the other channel
wall. The spectral predictions on hydraulic permeability, which are cross-

                                       22
validated against finite element simulations, show qualitative characteristics
not obtainable from asymptotic predictions available in the literature [9–
11]. For example, the spectral approach reveals that there is an intermediate
range of wavelengths, when the variation of permeability with amplitude
can be non-monotonic, demonstrating a mimimum. Similarly, as the spac-
ing between the corrugated and flat plates forming the channel is decreased a
minimum appears in the permeability of the patterned channel scaled relative
to that of an unpatterned channel with same pitch-averaged spacing between
the walls. Dimensional estimates of the minimum corrugation amplitude and
minimum height, as relevant to typical microfluidic configurations have been
provided in Section 3.
    The closed-form expressions for permeability appearing in [9] and effective
slip length appearing in [10] are shown to correspond to be formal asymp-
totic approximations of the spectral predictions, where only one mode in the
spectral representation of the flow-field contributes to the permeability. A
new spectrally-derived small-amplitude estimate of permeability (Eq.(14))
of a higher asymptotic order, is also provided. The limit of large channel
spacing, which under the scales chosen becomes equivalent to shear flow is
also explored and a new asymptotic prediction (Eq. (16)) is proposed, which
is more accurate for larger amplitude patterns than the predictions available
from Refs. [10, 11, 15].
    The spectral and finite-element predictions accurately match each other
closely, for all situations studied. The accuracy of asymptotic prediction of
Ref. [9] degrades significantly with increase in the amplitude and decrease in
the wavelength. Higher order asymptotic predictions derived in the current
study improve the numerical accuracy for moderately long and long wave-
lengths, but not for short wavelengths. Recent molecular dynamics results [1]
are also shown to be in close agreement with spectral results on shear flow,
if particular data sets from [1] with strong wall fluid interactions and sinu-
soidally ‘bent’ arrangement of atoms are chosen, and a correction inspired
by effective slip predictions in [11, 12] is made for intrinsic slip.
    New asymptotic relations on the effect of hydrodynamic slippage, such
as Eqs. (21a), Eqs. (22) and (23) are derived, which may be of relevance to
nanofluidic applications. A practically significant consequence of the anal-
ysis of slippage effects is that, if perfect slip is to be realized by complete
lubrication of one of the channel walls with a gas layer [40, 41], providing
lubrication on the wavier of the two walls may be advantageous from the
point of view of reduction of the channel hydraulic resistance. The formula-

                                      23
        tion is more general than past studies, since it allows for dissimilar degrees of
        slippage on the bounding walls. This aspect may be relevant to validation of
        future molecular dynamic simulations, where the extent of macroscopically
        averaged slippage depends both on the difference between the solid-liquid
        and liquid-solid inter-molecular potentials and the atomic configuration used
        to form the corrugations [45, 46].
            In the future work, a flow configuration where the flow occurs across the
        corrugations may be studied, which, when combined with the current results,
        will allow applications to mixing devices by virtue of the tensorial formal-
        ism of Refs. [3, 11]. It is anticipated that that the behaviour of hydraulic
        permeability explained here will useful on surface-texture and microchannel
        design for various applications e.g. passive mixers, pumps, flow detectors and
        separation devices for microfluidics [47–49], enhanced surface capture of bio-
        particles [6]. Naturally rough surfaces have more uneven shapes, whereas
        engineered surfaces commonly have corrugations composed of planar seg-
        ments. Therefore, generalizing the current work through Fourier analysis to
        more general shapes of corrugation than a sinusoid will be useful.

Appendix D. Additional details on numerical methods
    The finite element simulations were conducted in the commercial finite
element software COMSOL Multiphysics R . Grid independence up to three
decimal places in the predicted dimensionless permeabilities were assured all
over the parameter space explored in Section 3, which required a uniformly
sized triangular elements of size 0.02 times the average channel size. The ma-
trix inversion procedure was performed using the built-in function‘mldivide’
of the software Matlab R . Truncation of the equation system in Eq. (8) to
6 − 15 terms were sufficient to achieve three digit accuracy in permeability
predictions of the spectral method. In obtaining the permeability predictions
for 100 points between α = 0 and α = 1, the spectral solution was about
250-300 times faster than the finite-element-method simulation on a work-
station with 3.40 GHz processor. The root finding required for solving Eq.
(13) when the right hand side is unity, as discussed in Section 3 used the
built in function ‘fzero’ of Matlab R .
