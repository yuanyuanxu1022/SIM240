## I. INTRODUCTION

### ¶1　工艺背景与瓶颈所在

> **【图 1 已就位】** 本段与 ¶2 对应的装置/单胞示意图已完成：`figures/paper/Fig1_device_and_unitcell.png`（+pdf/svg），图注见 `figures/paper/Fig1_caption_and_record.md`。建议在 ¶1 末或 ¶2 首句处插 **“FIG. 1”** 引用。

Jet-and-flash imprint lithography (J-FIL) forms nanoscale patterns by dispensing picoliter resist droplets onto a substrate, pressing a relief-patterned, UV-transparent template into them, letting the resist fill the template features by capillary and viscous action, and then curing the film under UV light [1]. Because the process reproduces features by mechanical molding rather than optical imaging, its resolution is set by the template and is largely independent of the field size [1]. The step that limits throughput, however, is not the patterning but the filling: in a competitive process the resist must close a field in roughly one second, and this filling step consumes the largest share of the throughput budget [1].

Filling takes place in a geometry with two very different length scales. Laterally, a drop pattern spans millimetres to centimetres; vertically, the gap between the mold protrusions and the substrate closes from micrometres down to a few tens of nanometres, while the grooves themselves remain partly unfilled. Flow therefore occurs simultaneously in a thin annular film under the protrusions and in the deeper grooves between them, and the two paths offer very different resistance. Because the grooves run in one direction, this resistance is strongly directional: the resist spreads readily along the grooves and much more slowly across them. That directionality controls where the liquid front arrives first, how drops merge, and where gas is finally trapped — the origin of the void defects that limit yield [1,2].

### ¶2　介尺度模型与所依赖的闭合关系

Simulating the flow at the scale of a full field while resolving each nanometre feature is not feasible, and it is not necessary: the lateral scale is five orders of magnitude larger than the gap, so the lubrication approximation applies [2,3]. The template pattern is then represented not by resolved geometry but by an effective permeability tensor $\mathbf{K}$ that maps the macroscopic pressure gradient to the gap-averaged flux,
$$
\mathbf{q} = -\frac{\mathbf{K}}{\mu}\nabla_\parallel p , \qquad
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \nabla_\parallel\!\cdot\mathbf{q} = 0 ,
$$
where $\mu$ is the resist viscosity and $\nabla_\parallel$ is the in-plane gradient [2,4]. For a line-and-space pattern the two principal components of $\mathbf{K}$ follow from two elementary rules. Along the grooves the pressure gradient is common to all stripes, so the fluxes add: the cubic conductances are averaged arithmetically. Across the grooves the flux is common and the pressure drops add: the conductances are averaged harmonically. For a pattern of period $P$, groove width $w$ and groove depth $d$, with a residual gap $h$ under the protrusions, these rules give

$$
K_\parallel^{\rm mod}=\frac{1}{24}\left[(h+d)^3+h^3\right],
\qquad
K_\perp^{\rm mod}=\frac{1}{6}\,\frac{(h+d)^3\,h^3}{(h+d)^3+h^3},
\tag{1}
$$

as the transport coefficients per unit width. Equation (1) is the arithmetic–harmonic ("stripe") average, which is mathematically identical to the local cubic law routinely used for patterned channels [3]; the form employed in imprint modeling follows the same construction [2,4].

Equation (1) is attractive precisely because it is closed-form and requires no computation. It is, however, a one-dimensional construction applied to a two-dimensional cross-section, and it therefore discards two things. The arithmetic average treats each stripe as an independent parallel-plate channel, so it omits the shear on the vertical side walls of a groove. The harmonic average treats the protrusion and the groove as two resistors in series, so it omits the additional dissipation where the flow contracts into and expands out of the groove. Both omissions are largest exactly where the geometry is least slender — that is, at the deep grooves and thin gaps that occur near the end of an imprint cycle.

### ¶3　该闭合被广泛沿用，但其自身从未被定量检验

The closure has been adopted and extended repeatedly, but always as an input. Jain et al. [2] introduced the anisotropic permeability into a multi-drop spreading model for line-and-space templates; their comparison with experiment was qualitative, aimed at reproducing the observed directionality of spreading rather than at testing the magnitude of $\mathbf{K}$. Ban and Bonnecaze [4] subsequently showed that permeability alone cannot reproduce the measured flow front and added an anisotropic capillary-pressure boundary condition derived from Surface Evolver calculations; their key finding is that both anisotropic permeability and anisotropic capillary pressure are required, and their quantitative checks concern the capillary pressure, not the permeability. The same group later extended the framework to templates carrying several pattern regions [5], where a change of pattern orientation from 0° to 90° is handled by interchanging $k_{xx}$ and $k_{yy}$ — an operation that presumes the tensor transformation law holds.

In this line of work, and in the wider literature on patterned-template filling, the two components of $\mathbf{K}$ are carried through the analysis without independent verification against a resolved flow field. It should be emphasized that this is not a criticism of the modeling strategy: replacing several thousand sub-resolution features by an effective tensor is the only tractable route, and the strategy has been productive. The open question is narrower and entirely quantitative: for the rectangular grooves actually used in imprint templates, over the range of gaps encountered during filling, how large is the error of Eq. (1)? We note that numerically checking an averaged flow model against resolved solutions is itself an established practice in the tribology literature, where flow-factor and roughness-averaged models have been compared with Navier–Stokes and lattice-Boltzmann solutions [6,7]. The contribution sought here is therefore not the comparison methodology, but the error law for this particular geometry and its correction.

### ¶4　为什么解析路线补不上这一格

One might expect the gap to be filled analytically instead. For smoothly undulating walls such analyses exist and are accurate: spectral and domain-perturbation treatments yield closed-form permeability tensors for grooves of arbitrary depth [8,9], and they recover the classical asymptotic results in the thin- and thick-channel limits. These methods, however, rest on a Fourier representation of the wall shape, and the rectangular profile used in imprint templates is not represented by such a series in a usable way. Its Fourier coefficients decay only as $1/n$, so every spectral sum in which the correction is weighted by a single power of $n$ fails to converge — including $S_1=\sum_n n\,|g_n|^2$, on which the leading correction depends [8]. Dewangan and Datta accordingly state that their approach "does not specialize meaningfully to the rectangular topography, because of its jump discontinuity" [8]. The underlying difficulty is general. In a second-order domain-perturbation analysis of periodic surfaces, an ideal square-groove profile produces Fourier coefficients decaying as $1/m$, whereupon two diagonal entries of the mobility tensor diverge and the predicted slip becomes unbounded; the authors of that analysis conclude that the mobility formula "should not be used for surfaces with corners or vertical slopes", and sharpen this to the requirement that the surface be twice continuously differentiable [10].

This is a statement about a class of asymptotic methods, not a claim that no analytical description of a rectangular groove is possible — particular limits (very shallow corrugations, or the thin-film limit in which the groove merely adds a slip length) remain accessible to analysis. What it does mean is that for the geometry of interest here — grooves of depth comparable to the groove width, under a residual gap of the same order — the value of $\mathbf{K}$ cannot be obtained by perturbing around a smooth profile, and must instead be obtained by solving the flow in the actual geometry. The same conclusion follows from the failure of the slender-gap premise: with $h/P\approx0.31$ at a typical mid-cycle gap, and $d/w$ of order unity, neither the thin-gap nor the shallow-groove asymptote applies.

### ¶5　本文做什么、结论是什么

This paper therefore determines $\mathbf{K}$ directly. We solve the steady incompressible flow in an explicitly resolved periodic rectangular groove, using the lattice Boltzmann method on a grid fine enough to resolve the groove corners, and extract $K_\parallel$ and $K_\perp$ from the flux driven by a prescribed body force. The geometry is fixed to the imprint-relevant values $P=240$ nm and $w=120$ nm, with groove depth $d\in[25,120]$ nm and residual gap $h\in[25,125]$ nm; the closure of Eq. (1) is evaluated for the same geometry under the same driving and the same normalization, so that the comparison isolates the closure error rather than a difference in convention. Forty-eight cases were computed, each passing a set of numerical gates (mass drift, Mach number, window convergence, section consistency, and agreement with the analytic parallel-plate limit).

Three results emerge.

(i) *The closure is systematically optimistic, and the error is a function of geometry rather than of scale.* Both components are overestimated in every one of the thirty-six cases, and the overestimate grows monotonically as the groove becomes deeper relative to the gap and to its own width. Across that map the along-groove error rises from +10% to +205%, and the across-groove error from +8% to +54%. At the reference geometry ($h=75$ nm, $d=100$ nm) the closure predicts an anisotropy ratio $K_\parallel/K_\perp=3.70$ where the resolved flow gives $2.21$. The closure thus misrepresents not only the magnitude of the transport but the degree of directionality, which is the quantity that determines the shape of the filling front.

(ii) *The two components fail for different reasons, and the reasons are separable.* The along-groove error is governed mainly by $d/H_{\rm ref}$, where $H_{\rm ref}=h+\varphi d$ is the volume-equivalent height and $\varphi=w/P$; it is the side-wall shear omitted by the arithmetic average. The across-groove error is governed mainly by $d/w$ and is nearly independent of $h$; it is the step dissipation omitted by the series average. This separation is what makes a compact correction possible.

(iii) *The error can be removed by two dimensionless factors, and the correction transfers to geometries that were not used to fit it.* Multiplying Eq. (1) by
$$
F_\parallel=\frac{1}{1+1.7207\,(d/H_{\rm ref})^{1.8046}},
\qquad
F_\perp=\frac{1}{1+0.53\,(d/w)^{0.83}},
\tag{2}
$$
reduces the along-groove error from +66…+99% to within ±17% and the across-groove error from +34…+41% to within ±4%, on a holdout groove depth ($d=75$ nm) that was excluded from the fit. The stated validity domains are $h\in[50,125]$ nm with $d/w\le1$ for $F_\perp$ (±10%) and $d/w\le0.83$ for $F_\parallel$ (±20%), with a lower bound of $h=50$ nm; the departure of $F_\parallel$ at $d/w=1$ (±40%) marks where the side-wall correction itself begins to fail, and is reported rather than fitted away.

Two further checks support the interpretation of $\mathbf{K}$. First, grooves rotated by 30°, 45° and 60° reproduce both the tensor rotation law $K(\theta)=K_\parallel\cos^2\theta+K_\perp\sin^2\theta$ and the flow deflection $\tan\phi=(K_\perp/K_\parallel)\tan\theta$ to better than $10^{-4}$%, so treating $\mathbf{K}$ as a rank-two tensor — the premise implicit in interchanging components between orthogonal patterns [5] — is legitimate; what the closure gets wrong is the magnitude of the principal values, not the transformation. Second, increasing the viscosity fifteen-fold changes $\mathbf{K}$ by 0.0006% while scaling the flux by exactly $1/15$, confirming that $\mathbf{K}$ is a purely geometric quantity for a Newtonian resist, so that Eqs. (1) and (2) carry over to any Newtonian imprint resist without recomputation.

### ¶6　范围声明（主动划界）

The scope of the results is deliberately restricted, and the restrictions are worth stating at the outset. The calculations describe a fully filled, single-phase, Newtonian liquid in a fixed gap, so they represent the quasi-static limit of the filling process rather than the squeeze flow itself; the film is continuous, with no free surface, no trapped gas, and no contact line. Partial filling, wetting and capillary effects, gas entrapment and the rate dependence of the closing motion are outside the present treatment. This boundary is not incidental: once a gas phase occupies part of the pore space, the transport coefficient is no longer a purely geometric quantity but becomes a saturation-dependent relative permeability whose parameters require separate determination [11]. Within the stated scope, however, the results are independent of the resist viscosity and of the particular Newtonian resist used, and the correction of Eq. (2) applies region by region to templates carrying patterns of different depth — a case of practical interest, since a single template often mixes several feature sizes [5].

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines the geometry, the governing equations, the lattice Boltzmann formulation and the procedure used to extract $\mathbf{K}$. Section 3 reports the verification of the solver and the numerical uncertainty of the extracted coefficients. Section 4 presents the resolved transport coefficients over the parameter domain, and Section 5 compares them with the closure of Eq. (1) and derives Eq. (2). Section 6 discusses the physical origin of the two error mechanisms, and Section 7 concludes.
