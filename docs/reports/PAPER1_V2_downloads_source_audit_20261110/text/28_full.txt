Abstract
Reynolds’ lubrication theory is extended to capture two-fluid dynamics based
on relative permeability concepts used for multiphase flow in porous media. A
reduced-order model of gas dissolution is constructed by combining two-phase
lubrication theory with Henry’s law and analytic approximations of surface area
and diffusion length. The governing equations are solved as a thin finite-element
shell model that can be used to investigate process sensitivities to a variety
of technologically important parameters. Results are compared to those from
related computationally intensive alternative formulations and to experimental
flow visualizations to demonstrate its efficacy in modeling large area, many-drop
processes and capturing the gas dissolution behavior observed in practice.
Keywords: nanoimprint lithography, Reynolds lubrication, multiphase,
disperse, gas-liquid, dissolution

been rapidly advanced to meet these demands[4]. Pictured in Figure 1, J-FIL

    As this process has been used to produce patterned surfaces with feature
half-pitches as small as 4 nm[7], NIL has been recognized by the International
Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors as a possible next generation lithog-
raphy candidate[8]. Despite significant potential for these processes to advance


                                                2
manufacturing efficiency and precision, mitigation of print-defects remains a
significant challenge[1][8]. Management of the liquid drop coalescence in the
closing gap is critical to process optimization because trapped gas that does not
dissolve into the liquid results in non-fill defects and long dissolution dwell times
lead to reduced process throughput. This paper puts forth a mathematical for-
mulation based on multiphase flow concepts to address these problems. The
mathematical formulation is solved with the finite element method and allows
simulation of the merger of thousands of drops over tens of cm2 .
    Gas trapping and residual layer thickness (RLT) variations are key limiters
of process rate and yield, as trapped gas impedes feature filling and non-uniform
residual layers adversely effect subsequent etching processes. Physically consis-
tent mathematical models are essential to the development of predictive process
simulators. Simulator efficacy also depends on a robust and rapid numerical
solution method so that a wide range of process parameters can be explored
quickly.
    Computational analysis of the drop merger regime (i.e. Figure 1b) over areas
containing hundreds to tens of thousands of drops is very challenging in part due
to the multitude of interfaces and complex liquid-gas flow topology evolution
illustrated in Figure 2b. As the drops merge, filling of nano-size features in the
template draws liquid away from the gap over much shorter time scales than
those of dissolution and diffusion of micro-size gas bubbles within the gap.[9]
Moreover, capillary forces play a key role throughout the process, even resulting
in local flexure of the template and substrate[3].
    This study builds toward a complete predictive model to guide process de-
signs that minimize gas trapping and reduce variation in the residual layer.
Expedient model set up and short run times are also desirable so that design
and scale-up of the process can be realized. At a minimum, the model requires
first a suitable two-phase flow model, capable of scaling up to large flow areas
while capturing the aggregate micron-scale effects without computational com-
plexity. As such, trapping of micro-sized gas bubbles, subsequent gas dissolution
and diffusion through the liquid phase are the focus of this study. The effects
of capillary forces, structural mechanics and flow into template features will be
addressed in future work.
    Efforts to model the J-FIL process began with work by Colburn et al.[5] They
assessed template control schemes with an analytic approach that accounted for
both viscous resistance of the liquid phase and capillary forces as a function of
gap thickness, gap closure velocity, liquid material properties and drop pattern.
Reddy et al. [10] extended Colburn’s analytic model with a numerical finite
difference approach based on volume-of-fluid interface tracking to predict fill
times for non-uniform drop patterns. Roberts et al. [11] implemented a gen-
eral two-phase curvilinear lubrication formulation suitable for non-planar flow
domains typical of a J-FIL process with structurally flexible templates. Solving
the resulting equations with a finite-element based approach, they simulated
two-phase gap closure flow with level-set interface tracking. Moreover, they val-
idated their results against analytic models for single drop squeeze flow and by
accurately predicting evolution of the interface of a single drop as observed in


                                         3
experiment. Scale-up trials by the current authors to 100s and 1000s of drops
using the approach taken by Roberts et al. revealed the method required too
fine of a mesh to be practical for process design.
    More recently several authors have addressed fluid management in related
drop-dispensing, imprint lithography processes. A full three-dimensional, Navier-
Stokes, two-phase mixture flow approach was demonstrated by Peng et al.[12]
to simulate micro-size bubble formation in micro-size features using the finite
volume method. An innovative approach based on contact mechanics has been
developed by Taylor[13] that includes imbibition of liquid into the template
and the motion of liquid across the substrate and demonstrated to scale to
manufacturing areas. Jain et al. [14] have advanced a finite-difference, volume-
of-fluid (VOF) approach to address the J-FIL process that allows for large area,
(O(1000)) drop simulations, but ignores the gas phase. Nonetheless, he pro-
vides an analytical model that predicts an NIL parameter envelope wherein gas
physics are negligible[15].
    While useful process understanding has been gained from these works, no
approach accounts for the effects of gas trapping and pressure-driven gas dis-
solution with a continuum model over practical processing areas of O(10 cm2 ).
The approach put forth in this paper captures gas trapping and gas dissolu-
tion in a multiphase flow formulation that enables large scale (>1000 drops)
simulations suitable for iterative process design studies. The continuum ap-
proach is adapted from analyses of the Saffman-Taylor instability in oil-water
flows through fractured rocks[16] in which a two-fluid extension of Darcy flow
with relative permeability[17][18] is employed. The formulation is developed in
Section 2 utilizing multiphase concepts of saturation and relative permeability
augmented by a reduced-order gas dissolution model. Section 3 presents model
results, demonstrating simple verification and one envisioned application. Com-
parison between simulation and experimental flow visualization demonstrate the
model captures two-phase phenomena pertinent to prediction of gas dissolution
dwell times. Section 4 discusses the results and future work.

2. Model development
    In this section, a mathematical model is developed to capture two-phase
flow evolution from the moment at which the drops bridge the gap between
substrate and template to beyond the point at which all of the gas has either
escaped or been dissolved into the liquid, as depicted in Figure 2a. Figure
4 shows snapshots from an experimental flow visualization of the process as
captured with a high-speed camera. Model development follows observations
that as the gap thickness is decreased, flow is separated into three stages by
the events pictured in Figure 4. Formation of liquid bridges between upper
and lower surfaces marks the beginning of the first stage in which gap volume
decrease occurs by gas phase displacement. Contact of the drop edges with
one another (drop merger) marks the beginning of the gas compression stage.
During this stage, viscous forces in the thinning gap lead to large pressure
gradients in the liquid as it displaces the compressing gas. Pressure builds up


                                       4
in both gas and liquid phases to offset the squeeze forces, reducing the gap
closure rate and initiating the gas dissolution stage. During this stage, long
drop merger times due to increased resistance to liquid flow in very thin gaps
are exacerbated by the requirement that trapped gas must dissolve into the
liquid to avoid defects. Previous findings that smaller drops[19] lead to shorter
processing times suggests that solubility and the distance through which the
gas species must dissolve may play a role in dwell time reduction. Furthermore,
work demonstrating that dwell times are practically eliminated with condensible
gases[20] affirms that gas phase management plays a critical role in the process.
    An idealized model is developed in the remainder of this section assuming
drops of identical volume, Vd , are arrayed in a uniform grid across the substrate
in a square lattice with a pitch of λ, as shown in Figure 2b. Actual applications
often require array density variation over the device print area, as discussed in
Section 3.4.

2.1. Lubrication flow
    The small height of standing drops, O(1 μm), and large areas necessary
for manufacturing processes O(10 cm2 ) result in a flow domain of extreme as-
pect ratio, making a full three-dimensional multiphase flow model impractical.
Reynolds’ lubrication approximation[21] simplifies the Navier-Stokes equations,
dropping negligible terms when the gap thickness, h, is small compared to
the flow length, L, and the product of the ratio of inertial to viscous forces
(Reynolds number Re) with the ratio of gap thickness to flow length is small
(Re · h/L  1)[22]. Stokes[23] integrated the resulting three dimensional equa-
tions through the gap thickness assuming the surfaces have no relative, in-plane
motion. The resulting expression for the gap averaged velocity, v̄, as a func-
tion of the pressure gradient, ∇P , is used still to describe two-dimensional,
streamline flows in Hele-Shaw cells[24].

2.2. Mass balance
    An approach described by Brennen[25] as disperse, two-fluid flow is taken in
this development because it inherently allows multiple fluids to occupy the same
space and avoids the need to track explicit interfaces. Instead of tracking motion
of two fluids separately, the volume fractions are tracked by the “saturation” in
the gap, where a diffuse representation of the liquid volume is used as depicted
in figure 3.

employing two mass balance equations, one for each phase, adapted from phase
field models of Hele-Shaw flow[26]. Each mass balance is integrated through

2.5. Numerical implementation details
    The model equations (25 and 26) and associated boundary conditions are
discretized in space with the finite element method. The resulting nonlinear al-
gebraic equations are solved with Newton’s method and integrated in time with
the backward Euler method. This numerical system is implemented in the mul-
tiphysics finite element software Goma 6.0[32]4 . As the model was developed,
mass lumping, a small amount of artificial diffusion and a conservative clipping
method were found necessary to achieve stability of the transient simulations.
    Reformulating the time derivative matrices that result from the FEM dis-
cretization with the mass-lumping[33] procedure mitigates unbounded growth of

spurious oscillations in the vicinity of these regions cause the saturation to over-

spurious fields result in increasing non-physical behavior as solutions evolve in
time. Specifically, when the saturation is greater than unity, the gas phase mass
balance, Equation 26, drives the saturation higher. This non-physical behavior
is remedied with a computational switch that substitutes the gas equation with
an equation that acts to drive saturation back towards unity.

3. Results

    In this section the model is demonstrated at two levels, first on diffuse
drop patches and then on discrete drop patterns. An energy-based approach
to determining the specific relative permeability parameters of Equations 6-11
is discussed in Section 3.1 followed by discussion of sensitivity to key thermo-
physical parameters. A fine-grain, single-drop simulation exploring the limits of
accuracy of interface evolution and pressure field predictions demonstrates that
gas-liquid interfaces can still be captured effectively in Section 3.2. A simula-
tion that captures the drop merger process is compared with experimental flow
visualizations in Section 3.3. Finally, in Section 3.4, a coarse-grain simulation of
varying density drop arrays is used to illustrate the class of problems this model
was designed to support, and that the model is suitable for further advancement
in subsequent work.

4. Conclusion

    A model for two-phase flow in a closing gap that is suitable for simulating
drop merger of drop-dispensed nanoimprint lithography processes has been pre-
sented. The model is implemented in a continuum finite-element code using
two-dimensional shell elements to capture flow. This implementation enables
the application of lubrication theory to problems in which incompressible liquids
interact with compressible, dissolvable gases. The model demonstrates qualita-
tive prediction of closing gap drop merger by capturing evolution of interfaces
in addition to gas trapping/dissolution behaviors that are congruent with video
captured of the process.
    Conservation of mass and momentum are applied to each phase individu-
ally and interaction between phases occurs through the saturation-based rela-
tive permeabilities. This disperse, two-fluid flow model enables the simulation
of large area, multiphase lubrication processes by reducing the computational
complexity, and hence, computation time, with reduced-order models that allow
reduction of required spatial resolution. Variation of the dispensed drop pat-
tern, (i.e. spatial variation of drop size, lattice spacing and lattice type) can be
addressed in the dissolution model, however these variations are not reflected in
the relative permeability model, representing one of the many research pursuits
for future work.
    Additional avenues for future work include exploration of alternative gas
compression models that may be more appropriate for the length scales and
pressures involved in these NIL flows. As the dissolution model is formulated


                                             19
specifically for disperse, two-fluid flows, re-tailoring of the dissolution model may
improve the accuracy of discrete drop simulations, although the current model
adequately portrays gas trapping and subsequent gas dissolution when diffuse
interfaces are included. Incorporation with thin-shell structural mechanics will
extend simulation capabilities to enable prediction of residual layer thickness
variations driven by capillary action and coupled deformation of substrate and
template. To conclude, this work serves as a building block to be used in the
development of more advanced models that will enable predictive simulations
to guide the design of drop-dispensing nanoimprint processes.
